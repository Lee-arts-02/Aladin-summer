ABSTRACT
Immersive Virtual Reality (VR) is increasingly used in education to simulate real-world scenarios 
and support experiential learning. However, limited access to VR equipment remains a barrier to 
widespread adoption. This case study explores how VR can be effectively integrated into group 
learning by distributing roles among learners: a Performer, who interacts directly within the 
immersive VR environment, and Observers, who engage from outside the headset. Conducted in 
the context of a pedestrian safety training program, the study involved 45 second- and third-grade 
students in nine small groups. Through observation data analysis, the study identified distinct 
patterns in how Performers and Observers engaged in the VR simulation and reflection process. 
Performers exhibited real-time decision-making under pressure, while Observers demonstrated 
meta-cognitive awareness and collaborative sense-making. During the post-assessments, no sta -
tistically significant differences in safety knowledge or behavioral performance were found be -
tween Performers and Observers; the need for future studies with larger samples remained. The 
study identifies a four-stage guided reflection process that enabled groups to synthesize individual 
and shared experiences. Additionally, the study conceptualizes a Shared Mixed Reality learning 
space, where virtual and physical worlds are merged through peer collaboration and group 
reflection. These findings offer implications for future research and practice in VR-mediated 
group learning and reflection, highlighting VR's adaptability to resource-limited educational 
settings.

Following the VR-mediated group learning, students completed a knowledge test and a VR-based behavior assessment. The

3.5. Observation data analysis
This study employed an inductive data analysis approach to analyze the nine group cases. As a bottom-up approach, the inductive 
data analysis process allowed the data to speak for itself through the researcher's in-depth investigation of the raw data ( Bendassolli, 
2013 ). There were no preconceived hypotheses or theories used in the analysis process. In general, data analysis progressed in four 
steps adjusted by Braun and Clarke's (2006) thematic analysis procedure, including generating initial codes, identifying pattern codes, 
identifying themes, and defining themes. The four steps merge related procedures from the original six-step guide and focused on the 
theme generation. A qualitative data analysis program, NVivo Version 12, was used to store, organize, and manage the data collected 
in this study.
3.5.1. Step 1. Generate initial codes
The first step involved identifying and labeling initial behavior codes from video data, organized by group case. Three guiding 
decisions shaped the coding process (1) Codes were categorized by role (e.g., Performer , Observer , and Facilitator ): to support role-based 
analysis; (2) Coding units were short, distinct behavioral episodes due to the time-based nature of video data; and (3) Behaviors were 
coded separately for the group VR simulation and the group reflection, aligned with the research questions. An intercoder agreement 
procedure was used to ensure reliability of code generation ( Lincoln & Guba, 1985 ), with the first author conducted initial coding and 
the second author independently coded two randomly selected cases. Initial percent agreement was 85%, which increased to 97 – 98% 
following discussion and consensus resolution. The first author then coded the remaining cases, with a final spot-check confirming 97% 
agreement. As a result, 103 behavior codes were identified from the group VR simulation phase (28 for Performers, 75 for Observers, 
see Appendix B ), and 148 codes from group reflections phase (32 for Performers, 54 for Observers, 47 for the Facilitator, 15 for the 
group as a unit, see Appendix C ).
3.5.2. Step 2. Identify pattern codes
In Step 2, the initial codes were aggregated into pattern codes through iterative discussions between the two coders ( Bazeley, 
2013 ). This step aimed to synthesize the data into meaningful categories that captured the roles' actions across the learning phases. In 
the VR-mediated group simulation phase, the Performer's behaviors were grouped into five categories, the Observer's behaviors fell 
into six (see Appendix B ). Off-task behaviors were excluded to maintain focus on actions related to the research questions. During the 
group reflection phase, twelve pattern codes were identified, with Facilitator, Performer, and Observer behaviors each grouped into 
four categories (see Appendix C ). The initial codes and pattern identification set the foundation for further analysis and theme gen -
eration ( Yin, 2018 ).
3.5.3. Step 3. Identify and review themes
Guided by research questions, the researchers generate themes based on continuous discussion and negotiation in Step 3. Three 
themes emerged to represent the Performer's experience in VR-mediated group simulation, and six themes were identified to show the 
Observer's experience (RQ1). Four stages of group reflection emerged from the pattern codes, indicating how the Performer, Observer, 
and Facilitator collaboratively recall, analyze, and learn from the VR experiences (RQ2).
3.5.4. Step 4. Define themes and produce the report
The final step was defining and naming themes, which mainly focused on finalizing the themes and associated definitions. The four 
steps of thematic analysis, along with Appendices B and C , captured the progression from initial codes to patterns that informed the 
final theme generation. Based on continuous discussions and negotiations between researchers, this step yielded findings that

3.6. Outcome data analysis
Two types of learning outcomes were analyzed (RQ3). The knowledge test consisted of ten multiple-choice items on safe pedestrian 
behavior. Each student's score was calculated by dividing the number of correct answers by the total number of questions, resulting in a 
score between 0 (0% accuracy) and 1 (100% accuracy). For the behavioral outcome, a safe pedestrian behavior rubric was applied to 
assess students' actions at the 11 decision-making points in VR. Scores were determined by dividing the number of safe actions by the 
total decision-making points, yielding a safety score ranging from 0 (0% safe decision) to 1 (100% safe decisions).
Two trained raters independently scored the recorded behaviors using the rubric. Interrater reliability was assessed using Cohen's 
kappa, indicating high agreement between raters ( k = 0.94, p < .001). The few discrepant decision-making points were resolved 
through a second review and discussion until consensus was reached, and the agreed final pedestrian performance scores were used for 
data analysis.
Given the VR-mediated group learning setting, the four Observers within each group shared the same VR simulation and group 
reflection experience. Analyses were therefore conducted at the group level to preserve the independent assumption. The mean 
learning outcome scores of the four Observers were computed and paired with the corresponding Performer's scores, yielding nine 
matched pairs for analysis ( n = 9). Wilcoxon signed-ranked tests with continuity correction were then used to compare the learning 
outcomes between the Performer and Observer roles. ( Field, 2013 ). Data analysis was conducted using R (version 4.4.0; R Core Team, 
2024 ) in the RStudio integrated development environment (version 2024.4.1.748; Posit Team, 2024 ).
4. Results
4.1. RQ1: Role-based experiences in VR-mediated group simulation: Performer versus observer
4.1.1. Performer in the virtual spotlight
In the VR-mediated group simulation, the Performer's behaviors were grouped into five categories: (a) Spontaneous Action, (b) 
Independent (Conscious) Action, (c) Responsive Action, (d) Influenced Action, and (e) Active Interaction with Others (see Appendix B ). 
Three themes emerged from the pattern codes to capture the Performer's experience. Firstly, the Performer's role involved both benefits 
and drawbacks. Their Independent Actions (e.g., interact with objects in VR) indicated the Performer's access and control over the 
virtual environment, while certain Spontaneous and Responsive Actions revealed a loss of awareness of physical surroundings (e.g., 
lose balance). Meanwhile, the role of Performer carried implicit responsibilities. Their behaviors were largely driven by the goal of 
completing the VR tasks in the presence of others, whether through Spontaneous Actions (e.g., respond to the VR stimuli), Conscious 
Action (e.g., make decisions), or Active Interaction with Others (e.g., seek help). Furthermore, the Performer actions and decision- 
making in VR were affected by Observers in the group setting. Their behaviors were shaped both unconsciously (e.g., spontaneous 
reaction to directions) and consciously (e.g., actively ask for others' suggestion).
Empowerment. The Performer navigated a dynamic landscape of gains and losses inherent in immersive VR technology. They 
enjoyed full access to multimodal experiences and hold significant control over the virtual world. Accordingly, the Performer role 
empowered the student to make decisions in VR to shape the outcomes and directions of the virtual scenarios. However, this immersion 
came at the cost of disconnecting from the physical world. The Performer's actions in Group Case #3 demonstrated her autonomy in 
VR, while also revealing reduced awareness of her physical surroundings: 
… as the pedestrian traffic light turned to green in the virtual city, the Performer immediately initiated movement towards the 
crossroad. Stepping forward with purpose, she intent to cross the road within the simulated environment. In her attempt to 
navigate the virtual environment, she momentarily lost balance, prompting her to regain stability in the physical world … as she 
moved in the virtual city, physically she was moving toward the edge of the VR working station and almost hit a chair. The 
safety guide directed her into the center of the zone. (extraction and description of the Performer ’ s actions from Group Case #3)
Responsibility. With the privilege of access and control in VR, the Performer role took unspoken responsibilities that required 
efforts and focus to fulfill. When a student volunteered or was assigned to this role, they had to wear the VR headset and use controllers 
to interact with the virtual world. The role carried to a tacit expectation: completing tasks in the VR program with their peers 
observing. This expectation led to a list of duties and requirements. The student needed to orient and adjust themself in the virtual 
environments, overcoming any difficulties caused by the setting (e.g., can't see one's physical environment and learning how to 
navigate the virtual space). To complete tasks in the virtual city, the Performer needed to apply certain pedestrian knowledge and 
make decisions in complex situations. Importantly, the Performer was not isolated in their actions; they were accompanied by a safety 
guide, four peer Observers from the same class, and researchers collecting data. The presence of these individuals could lead to un -
predictable factors, such as pressure, distraction, or assistance, affecting the Performer's experience. For instance, in Group Case #1, 
one Performer struggled during the task: 
… suddenly, the Performer couldn ’ t move forward in the virtual city. She tried stepping forward and waving her arms as 
directed, but she couldn ’ t get into the crosswalk even though the traffic light was green. Her peers were chatting, and the safety 
guide tried to direct her somewhere she couldn ’ t see in the physical world. The guide left to check the VR program, leaving her 
standing still, facing her peers with the headset on. The four Observers chatted and laughed and turned silent …

Influence. With peer Observers involved, the Performer's actions and decision-making were inevitably influenced. Since the 
learning group consisted of classmates, pre-existing social relationships could affect the group dynamic. The mere presence of Ob -
servers could lead to changes in the Performer's behavior, as they became more self-conscious and aware of being watched. This 
awareness can cause subtle shifts in the Performer's actions. Moreover, the Performer often actively seek input from Observers, inviting 
them to contribute to their decision-making process. For example, in Group Case #3, the Performer asked for confirmation from the 
Observers before acting, “can I go (cross the road) now?” Beyond invited involvement, Observers could directly influence the Per -
former's decisions by offering advice or sharing information. In Group Case #5, Observers guided the Performer, suggesting specific 
actions that reflected their own ideas and preferences, “go forward, you'll see a parking lot!” The presence of multiple Observers can 
also create additional layers of intervention, as their conversations or interactions may indirectly impact the Performer's choices. For 
instance, the Performer's decision-making can be distracted by the discussions happening between Observers, further complicating 
their experience in the simulation.
4.1.2. Observers in the virtual periphery
The Observer's behaviors fell into six categories: (a) Engaged Observation, (b) Spontaneous Action, (c) Responsive Action, (d) 
Proactive Action, (e) Interaction with Performer, and (f) Interaction with Other Observers (see Appendix B for details). Six themes 
emerged from the pattern codes to represent the Observer's experience. Specifically, the Observer obtained the sense of mental im -
mersion through engaged observations (e.g., the Observer reacts spontaneously to the virtual stimuli). The Observer interacted with 
the virtual environment through Interaction with Performer. By helping the Performer complete the VR tasks, the Observer gained a 
sense of ownership in VR experience (e.g., think actively about solving problems in VR, prevent others from disturbing the Performer). 
Furthermore, the Observer possessed the flexibility to shift perspectives between the real and virtual worlds. With multiple observers 
in the group settings, the Observer played different roles and was influenced by the presence and actions of other Observers.
Engagement. The Observers were found highly engaged in the VR simulation even though they didn't have direct access to VR. The 
Observer obtained a sense of mental immersion in VR through engaged observation. Observers displayed spontaneous reactions to the 
virtual environment stimuli, using body language to simulate movements (e.g., Group Case #1, #6, the Observers stand up to step 
forward and wave hands, imitating the moving actions in VR). Additionally, they mentally placed themselves in the VR environment, 
often verbalizing thoughts as if they were the ones acting in VR. For example, one Observer remarked, “only if I can walk faster (in VR)” 
while watching the Performer cross a road (Group Case #2).
Influence. Another approach the Observer used to engage with the virtual world was by influencing the Performer's actions. 
Although they had no direct control over the VR program, the Observer shaped the experience by verbally guiding the Performer. They 
often provided real-time directions or suggestions to influence decision-making, actively participating in the task completion. For 
example, in Group Case #9, Observers urged the Performer to “run! Go faster, faster!” before the traffic light changed, showcasing how 
their input altered the course of the simulation. This indirect interaction allowed Observers to engage meaningfully with the VR 
content, despite not being physically immersed.
Role Shifting. Additionally, the Observer benefited from the ability to shift perspectives between the virtual and real worlds. Unlike 
the Performer who was fully immersed in VR, the Observer watched the simulation on a desktop while remaining aware of their 
physical surroundings. This awareness allowed them to discuss strategies with peers and plan their own actions in VR from outside the 
headset. By observing the Performer's actions and mentally engaging with the task, they could actively participate through indirect 
influence while imagining their future performance in similar situations. In Group Case #7, Observers demonstrated their engagement 
by combining mental immersion, influencing the Performer's actions, and shifting perspectives between the virtual and real worlds. 
… As the Performer approached the road, he paused to check both sides for approaching vehicles. Observing the VR display 
from the desktop, one Observer commented, “I would look up to check the traffic signal.” In response, the Performer looked up, 
and the traffic light shown green, signaling it was safe to cross … (Researcher memo: this moment exemplifies how the Ob -
server’s input influenced the Performer’s actions, bridging their own perspective with the virtual world and actively engaging in 
the simulation process).
Multiple Roles. Though all engaged in group VR simulation, the Observers were different. The analysis revealed that Observers 
played varied roles throughout the simulation. Some were passive bystanders, merely watching without much involvement (Group 
Case #4), while others imagined themselves in the simulation, mimicking actions as if they were co-players (Group Case #2). Certain 
Observers acted as directors, influencing the Performer's actions to reflect their preferences (Group Case #7), while others disrupted 
the process with distracting behaviors (Group Case #3). Many also took on the role of helpers, guiding the Performer to successfully 
complete the assignment (Group Case #9).
Shared Ownership. Notably, the Observer developed a sense of ownership by helping the Performer accomplish the assignment. 
Although there was no pre-determined rule regarding how the group worked together, most group cases showed collaboration in 
completing the simulation tasks. Specifically, the Observer supported the Performer in completing the assignment by providing 
suggestions/directions (e.g., Group Case #9), encouragement (e.g., Group Case #2), and efforts to prevent disruption and promote the 
process (e.g., Group Case #3). Finishing the VR simulation became not merely the Performer's mission but a shared and collaborative 
experience for the Observers.
Interaction Between Observers. Since there was more than one Observer in a group, Observers interacted with each other as well as 
with the Performer. Different interactive behaviors were found between Observers, suggesting the Observer's experience was also 
influenced by others who shared the same role in the group. For example, Observers were found to share their observed information 
and opinions with each other. Furthermore, when an Observer directs others' attention to their focus, he influences other Observers'

4.1.3. Behavioral comparison of two roles in VR-mediated group simulation
Based on the identified pattern codes of the Performer's and Observer's actions ( Appendix B ), shared behavioral patterns across both 
roles were subsequently compared. Accordingly, five categories of behavioral patterns were identified to compare the two roles' ac -
tions in VR-mediated group simulation, including (a) Spontaneous Action, (b) Responsive Action, (c) Independent/Proactive Action, 
(d) Influenced Action, and (e) Interactive Action (see Table 1 ).
Although the Performer and Observer had different access to the VR program—one was fully immersed in VR with control, and the 
other saw the virtual world from projection—they both had active and responsive actions to the virtual world. The Performer actively 
engaged with and responded to virtual objects to accomplish tasks within the VR program, while the Observer reacted to the virtual 
environment indirectly, often directing the Performer to act as they themselves might in VR. This dynamic led Observers to partially 
perceive the Performer as their avatar, shaping the experience through guidance and feedback. Accordingly, both roles displayed 
spontaneous, responsive, active decision-making actions toward the tasks in VR. Meanwhile, interactive behaviors between the two 
roles also happened in VR-mediated group learning, where they were connected through the real-time projection of the VR program 
and free communication between each other. Both roles were aware of their role differences compared to others in the group, and they 
were motivated and cognitively engaged in problem-solving within the VR program. Additionally, both roles’ behaviors and actions 
were influenced by others, resulting in a co-constructed and shared experience in the group rather than an independent one.
The difference between the two roles' behaviors was also apparent. Since the Performer was the only person simulated in VR, the 
role was exposed more to the virtual world, triggering various spontaneous actions during the simulation process. Meanwhile, since the 
Performer had the control and implicit duty to accomplish tasks in VR, the role enacted more conscious and independent actions than 
the Observer. Furthermore, the Performer was the only person in the group who could actually make an impact on the virtual world, 
therefore, endured others' presence and active involvement to enact influenced behaviors. In contrast, the Observer's behaviors were 
based upon their notice of the Performer's actions and the virtual world projected on the screen. The two roles had different goals when 
they joined the group with assigned roles and resources, and they used various means to enact their thoughts and impact the virtual 
environment.
4.2. RQ2: Group reflection on the VR-mediated group experience
The inductive data analysis identified four distinct stages of group reflection, indicating how the Performer and Observers reflected 
on their unique and shared experiences with the guidance of the Facilitator. The three roles shown specific behaviors during each 
stage. These patterns aligned closely across roles and were guided by the Facilitator's scaffolding. Final emerged themes with 12 role- 
specific actions (four core actions by three roles) is presented in Table 2 (see Appendix C for initial codes). Specifically, the first stage, 
Guided Retrospection and Description of Experience, focused on reviewing critical experiences for further analysis. The Performer and 
Observer shared their individual and collective experience, and the Facilitator provided guidance for the group to recall critical details 
and their thoughts during the experience. The second stage, Guided Analysis and Evaluation of Experience, suggested the group's 
deliberation on their VR experience. The Performer and Observer evaluated and analyzed their individual and shared VR experiences 
with the support of the Facilitator. The third stage, Guided Group Exploration and Discussion, indicated the group's guided teamwork 
in examining and reconsidering their VR simulation experience. The final stage is Learning and Conclusion, which indicated the 
group's meaning-making and future decisions from the group reflection. These stages demonstrate that the group, despite having 
different roles, engaged in a structured and collaborative process to reflect on both immersive and observational experiences. While 
these stages provide partial answers to RQ2, additional findings from the analysis offer valuable insights into the dynamics of group

4.2.1. Facilitator in group reflection
Essential Role. The importance of the Facilitator in guiding group reflection became evident, especially for students lacking 
experience in teamwork and reflective practices. The Facilitator led the reflection by asking prompt questions and providing feedback, 
ensuring students stayed focused on critical experiences. Additionally, the Facilitator shown three critical skills: knowledge of the 
subject matter being reflected upon, an understanding of reflective processes to foster meaningful discussions, and the ability to create 
a supportive environment for collaboration and teamwork. The direct and observational experiences were connected due to the Fa -
cilitator's deliberate guidance.
Challenging Position. Practical challenges were identified in the Facilitator's role. One challenge is that students may become 
overly dependent on the Facilitator's guidance, which can limit their autonomy and active participation in the reflection process. 
Additionally, the Facilitator's position of authority, particularly when there is power or knowledge imbalances, can affect group dy -
namics and hinder open dialogue. Lastly, managing group reflection, especially in larger groups, proved demanding. The Facilitator 
found it difficult to balance multiple students' inputs, handle conflicts, and maintain focus on various topics, as demonstrated by the 
memo. 
I think I was playing an essential role in the group reflection as the students relied on my guidance to review and analyze their 
experiences … it was stressful when five children in the group talked to me at the same time, especially when they were focusing 
on different things. They all wanted my attention, but I could only lead one topic at a time. I found myself forgetting to follow up 
with other topics after letting one child share their opinion first, let alone handling conflicts or disagreements between different 
individuals (Facilitator’s memo and reflecting notes).
4.2.2. Performer in group reflection
Social Pressure. The group reflection was a different experience for the Performer compared to VR simulation. The Performer 
experienced social pressure due to being the focus of the reflection process, as their actions were central to the discussion. This 
spotlight could cause discomfort, especially when Observers were critical or judgmental. For example, in Group Case #2, the Observers 
critiqued the Performer's risky behavior in VR during their group debriefing, leading the Performer to withdraw emotionally and 
become less engaged in the discussion. The Facilitator had to intervene to reintegrate the Performer into the group dialogue. Addi -
tionally, Observers often overlooked the Performer's retrospection, interrupting or answering questions on their behalf, which 
diminished the Performer's opportunity to reflect openly.
Cognitive Load. The Performer faced a high cognitive load from being fully immersed in VR, which might hinder their ability to 
recall their experiences accurately for meaningful reflection during the discussion. Immersive environments provided complex, 
multimodal stimuli, making it difficult for the Performer to focus on key details during reflection. In Group Case #5, for instance, the 
Performer missed critical cues noticed by the Observers. Furthermore, Performers sometimes struggled to remember details from the 
VR experience, as seen when one Performer in Group Case #2 was unable to recall specific events, despite wanting to contribute to the 
discussion, “I think I remember it, but I just can't recall it at the moment.” This highlights the cognitive demands placed on the 
Performer during both the VR simulation and the reflection process. A few extractions from the group reflection videos show the 
challenges the Performer faced:

4.2.3. Observers in group reflection
Ownership. The Observer got a unique position in group reflection. They shared a sense of ownership over the VR simulation 
experience, though they were not the one simulating. They often interrupt the Performer to answer questions or share details from 
their own perspective. For example, in Group Case #6, an Observer used first-person language to describe the Performer's actions, 
saying, “ I didn't see the traffic light, so I thought it was okay to cross. ” Observers also fill in details the Performer missed, such as when 
one Observer noted, “ It was flashing before you arrived at the corner ” (Group Case #5). Additionally, they challenge the Performer's 
recollections, as seen when an Observer corrected the Performer's memory of a school bus in Group Case #2.
Role Awareness. Even though showing ownership on the VR experience, the Observer remained aware of the differences between 
their role and the Performer's. Observers often compared themselves to the Performer, judging their actions and offering alternative 
decisions. For example, when the Performer stated that he would cross the road when the vehicles had stopped, the Observer dis -
agreed, stating, “ I would not do that. It is still risky. I would check the traffic light and vehicles. ” (Group Case #4). While discussing the 
Performer's behaviors, the Observer planned for their own VR experience. For instance, when the group debated whether the Per -
former's action was safe in VR, one Observer looked around and said aloud, “ I will do something like this …” (Group Case #2).
Social Dynamics. Notably, there was a lack of social rules governing how Observers interact with others in the group. They 
frequently interrupted both the Performer and other participants to share their perspectives, often without realizing the impact. For 
example, in Group Case #1, when the Facilitator asked the Performer about her actions in a particular situation, the Observer 
interrupted and said, “ She was waiting there because the traffic light showed red. ” Observers also struggled to handle differing 
opinions, sometimes causing tension, as in Group Case #2 when an Observer's comments upset the Performer. In some cases, Observers 
even retracted their suggestions after being challenged by peers (Group Case #3). For example, one Observer explained their thought 
process for a specific situation, stating, “ I would cross the road when the flashing light comes on. ” Another Observer disagreed, 
questioning, “ Who said that? ” In response, the first Observer denied their statement, claiming, “ I didn't say that. ”
4.3. RQ3: Learning outcomes from the VR-mediated group learning
4.3.1. The Performers' and observers ’ knowledge test
Both the Performer and Observer groups completed a knowledge test following the VR-mediated group learning to examine 
learning outcome related to pedestrian knowledge acquisition. Given the design of the group learning, four Observers within each 
group case shared the same VR simulation and group reflection experience. Therefore, analyses were conducted at the group level to 
preserve the independent assumption. For each of the nine group cases, the mean knowledge test score of the four Observers was 
computed and paired with the corresponding Performer's score, yielding nine matched pairs for analysis ( n = 9). A Wilcoxon signed- 
ranked test with continuity correction was used to examine the difference between the Performer and Observer groups' learning 
outcome, considering the small sample size limits the reliability of the normality assumption required for parametric testing ( Field, 
2013 ).
The Wilcoxon signed-ranked test revealed no statistically significant difference in knowledge test between the Performer ( M = 0.86, 
SD = 0.13) and Observer groups ( M = 0.75, SD = 0.23), V = 30.5, p = .093, pseudomedian = 0.125, 95% CI [   0.050, 0.288]. The 
positive pseudomedian, the direction of the V statistic, and a confidence interval leaning predominantly positive collectively suggested 
a pattern in which Performers tended to score higher than the mean of their groups ’ Observers across the nine groups. Although the 
difference did not reach statistical significance at α = .05, a large effect size was observed ( r = 0.574, 95% CI [0.08, 0.89]). This suggest 
that the non-significant p-value is likely attributable to insufficient statistical power given the small sample of nine paired groups 
( Cumming, 2014 ). These findings indicate that while no statistically significant differences in knowledge acquisition were found in this 
study, a potential advantage for Performers that requires further investigation with larger samples.
4.3.2. The Performers' and Observers ’ behavior performance in VR
Both the Performer and Observer groups completed a VR-based behavior performance assessment following VR-mediated group 
learning to examine their application of pedestrian safety behaviors. As analyses were conducted at the group level, the mean behavior 
performance score of the four Observers within each group was computed and paired with the corresponding Performer's score, 
yielding nine matched pairs ( n = 9). A Wilcoxon signed-ranked test with continuity correction was used to examine the difference 
between the Performer and Observer groups' behavior performance in VR after the group learning ( Field, 2013 ).
The Wilcoxon signed-rank test revealed no statistically significant difference in behavior performance between the Performer ( M =
0.73, SD = 0.22) and Observer ( M = 0.77, SD = 0.11) groups, V = 15, p = .726, pseudomedian =   0.023, 95% CI [   0.223, 0.125]. The 
near-zero pseudomedian and a confidence interval symmetrically crossing zero indicated no consistent directional pattern observed 
across the nine groups, suggesting that Performers and Observers did not demonstrate statistically significant difference in pedestrian 
behavior assessment. Meanwhile, a small effect size was observed ( r = 0.119, 95% CI [0.00, 0.72]), indicating a negligible difference 
between the two roles in behavior performance.
While the results indicated no statistically significant differences in behavior performance between Performer and Observer groups, 
a non-significant p-value does not provide evidence that the two groups are equivalent ( Lakens, 2017 ). The confidence interval 
crossing zero, combined with the small sample size, indicates that the current data cannot definitively determine whether the groups 
are truly equivalent or meaningfully different. However, the small effect size suggests that any practical difference, if present, is likely 
minimal.

4.3.3. The Performer ’ and Observer's satisfaction and perceived learning experience
The closing interviews with the forty-five students provided valuable insights into their satisfaction and perceived learning ex -
periences during the VR-mediated group learning activities. Overall, both Performers and Observers expressed positive feedback, 
enjoying the VR experience and recognizing its relevance to real-life situations.
For Performers, six out of nine preferred simulating the VR program with peers present, citing the support and suggestions they 
received during the activity. As one Performer shared, “ I preferred group experience when others were around. They (the Observers) 
could give me suggestions like when I didn't notice the traffic light, they told me to go ” (Group Case #3). In contrast, three Performers 
expressed a preference for experiencing the VR alone, mainly due to concerns about peer interruption and discomfort of being observed 
by others. For example, one Performer mentioned, “ I prefer experiencing the VR by myself. They would laugh at me. I don't like that ” 
(Group Case #4). However, all Performers reported enjoying the group reflection, valuing the opportunity to discuss the VR experience 
with their peers. Many highlighted the benefits of peer interaction, both in terms of fun and learning. One Performer noted, “ I like 
discussing the experience with others because they would help me correct my mistakes (risky behaviors). I like to reflect with others 
because they would also learn things ” (Group Case #2).
Observers reported similar experiences to Performers, with twenty-four indicating that they preferred engaging with the VR 
program themselves rather than observing. One Observer explained, “ I prefer playing the program by myself than observing. It will be 
more fun. I like the scenes (in VR). I can do whatever I want ” (Group Case #9). Five Observers preferred watching others due to 
personal discomfort with VR, citing reasons such as dizziness or fear. For example, one Observer shared, “ I dare not play the VR 
program. I am afraid of being hit (by cars in the virtual world) ” (Group Case #4).
5. Discussion and conclusion
This study investigated how learners engage, reflect, and construct knowledge in VR-mediated group learning through distinct 
roles: the Performer immersed in simulation and the Observer interacting from the periphery. By analyzing nine group cases, we 
explored the dynamics between these roles during simulation and reflection. The findings offered insights into how different forms of 
participation shape learning experiences in immersive environments.
5.1. The discovery of immersive VR-mediated group learning space
The immersive VR-mediated learning groups were formed by two roles with different resources and constraints. The Performers' 
and Observers' experiences in such a group setting can be explained using a learning space combining different types of realities (see 
Fig. 3 ). The blue area (A) represents the exclusive Virtual Reality, where Performers navigate without any physical world influence. In 
the nine group cases, Performers navigated in exclusive Virtual Reality when they received no intervention from their physical sur -
roundings. For example, their Spontaneous and Responsive Actions to the virtual environment. In contrast, the exclusive Physical 
Reality indicates the space Observers positioned without knowledge of the virtual environment (the yellow area, C). The Observers ’ 
Proactive, Responsive, and Interactive behaviors occurred in physical reality with others from the physical world.
Importantly, the group setting created a novel learning space where the boundaries between the virtual and physical worlds blurred 
by a projected display. Observers in the physical environment were able to watch and understand what Performers were experiencing 
in VR. Thus, students from virtual and physical realities were able to communicate and collaborate. We define the overlapped space as

Shared Mixed Reality (SMR) in this study (the green area, B). For example, in Group Case #9, the Performer completed VR tasks in the 
presence of Observers. In SMR, the Performer was immersed in the virtual world while physically sharing the same space with peers. 
The Observers watched the Performer's actions and corresponding consequences in VR via a projected display. When the traffic light in 
the virtual scene turned red, the Observers called out, “Run! Go faster, faster!”, prompting the Performer to respond accordingly. In this 
interaction, the Performer (re)acted consciously and unconsciously to others from their physical surroundings. As a results, the Ob -
servers impacted the virtual space by actively influencing the Performer's actions in VR. Thus, the SMR space provided a chance for 
those who were not immersed in VR to enact their thoughts in the virtual world. Accordingly, dynamic group interactions occurred due 
to the co-existence and overlapping of the virtual and physical spaces.
The discovery of immersive VR-mediated learning space offers several implications for future research. First, the study identified 
SMR as a shared space where learners exchange their physical and virtual experiences to achieve mutual awareness and interaction. 
Such a definition aligns with Benford et al.’s (1998) suggestion of Mixed Reality (MR), defining it as a shared space based on con -
structing and integrating transparent boundaries between real and virtual spaces. However, as Speicher et al. (2019) found from their 
interview with experts and literature review, the definition of MR is highly fragmented and diverse. Multiple terms are found to be used 
interchangeably with MR (e.g., augmented reality, virtual reality), and the MR experience is not clearly described and defined in 
studies. Accordingly, there is a need for further research to better define and characterize the notion of MR experiences. For example, 
unlike forms of MR that blend physical and virtual worlds for individuals, SMR is a specific form of MR that emphasizes interaction and 
mutual awareness between two or more individuals who have similar yet distinct experiences from virtual and physical realities. 
Second, the study highlights the potential of collaborative learning in VR-mediated environments, where one student engages in VR 
while others observe. This setup offers effective and interactive learning opportunities despite the limited availability of VR tech -
nology. The finding of SMR contributes to the interactions and exchanges of experience across physical and virtual experiences. 
Further research is needed to explore the nature of collaboration within these environments, particularly the different layers of group 
and sub-group interactions.
The VR-mediated group learning space also has some implications for instructional design. Specifically, the study revealed the 
importance of authentic and engaged learning situations, rather than the direct use of VR technology, in triggering mental immersion 
and cognitive engagement. Therefore, schools with limited resources of immersive VR equipment and resources can organize group 
learning activities to benefit more students with the observational learning mode. Additionally, the practice of VR-mediated group 
learning requires further considerations of effective scaffolding. Due to the division of labor and combination of multiple realities in 
VR-mediated group learning, students in such a learning environment need support to overcome challenges and collaborate with 
others. For example, instead of focusing solely on virtual or physical experiences, educators and instructional designers could 
intentionally design SMR experiences to connect and support learners in resource-demanding environments. This study examined the 
intervention of guided group reflection on students’ interactions and learning outcomes, while more instructional strategies need to be 
designed and examined to support collaborative learning in VR-mediated group settings.
5.2. The four stages of group reflection
The study demonstrates four stages of group reflection, including (guided) retrospection and description of the experience, (guided) 
analysis and evaluation of the experience, (guided) group exploration and discussion, and (guided) learning and conclusion. 
Noticeably, the four stages are not sequential but interrelated. Fig. 4 suggests the relationship between the group reflection stages 
found in this study. Since group reflection is an activity accomplished by a group of learners rather than an individual, group in -
teractions and communications play a central role in the process. Therefore (Guided) Group Exploration and Discussion (e.g., Facil -
itator invites Performer or Observer to share, discuss, or debate on their opinions), co-exists with and influences the other three stages 
of group reflection. The (Guided) Retrospection and Description of the experience (e.g., Facilitator asks Performer or Observer to recall 
what they saw/heard/thought) is the first step in the group reflection process, which allows the group to share their individual and 
shared impressions on the experience. The (Guided) Analysis and Evaluation of the experience (e.g., Facilitator prompts Performer or 
Observer to interpret/analyze what they saw/heard/thought) is a critical stage in the group reflection process, helping the group 
examine and reconsider their knowledge and behaviors during the experience. Finally, the (Guided) Learning and Conclusion (e.g., 
Facilitator supports Performer or Observer to summarize/conclude what they learn) is the last stage in the process, which suggests the 
results of group reflection, such as new understanding and plan for future behaviors.
Notably, the finding of the four stages of group reflection provides experimental support for the statement that reflection proceeds 
with systematic phases rather than a causal thinking process ( Yang & Choi, 2023 ). Reflection has been disentangled into multiple 
stages or phases by a number of scholars ( Atkins & Murphy, 1993 ; Bain et al., 2002 ; Boud et al., 1985 ; Boyd & Fales, 1983 ; Dewey, 
1933 ; Gibbs, 1988 ; Korthagen & Vasalos, 2005 ; Rodgers, 2002 ). The group reflection stages found in this study validate the general

The findings of the four group reflection stages in this study have some implications for group reflection research and practice. 
Specifically, the four stages of (guided) group reflection require further investigation and validation in different learning contexts. 
Findings revealed in this specific study need further investigation in different contexts for generalization. This study also confirmed a 
couple of theoretical justifications for group reflection. For instance, the findings revealed that learners had varying focal points during 
their VR simulation experiences, which facilitated analysis on collective experiences. Moreover, the study demonstrated that in -
dividuals could identify their misconceptions and overcome limitations through group reflection. These findings align with the 
theoretical perspectives that suggest group environments offer opportunities for reflection and can assist individuals in achieving what 
they may not be able to accomplish alone ( Eshuis et al., 2019 ; Johnson & Johnson, 1999 ; Vygotsky, 1978 ).
The findings from the group reflection stage also have implications for instructional design and reflective practice. The study 
confirmed the need for scaffolding to support group reflection, particularly for young learners who may lack collaboration and 
reflection skills. Future practices should focus on strategies to guide different stages of group reflection. Additionally, the stages of 
group reflection emphasized the importance of Guided Group Exploration and Collaboration, suggesting that teachers should prioritize 
practical facilitation strategies. Challenges such as a lack of shared understanding or social rules can hinder reflection, so educators 
could create strategies to foster open, collaborative environments that support meaningful group reflection.
5.3. Similar experiences and learning outcomes obtained by different roles
The study found that learners achieved similar experiences and learning outcomes despite their different access to VR. Both 
Performers and Observers were mentally immersed, cognitively engaged, and demonstrated motivation, agency, and ownership in 
problem-solving. Despite their role differences, both groups were aware of the team dynamic, actively interacting with peers and 
influenced by each other's presence. This suggests that VR-mediated group learning, where peers observe one student's VR simulation, 
can provides immersive and engaging experiences for all participants regardless of role. Post-activity assessments indicated no sta -
tistically significant differences in safety knowledge or behavioral performance between Performers and Observers, although the large 
effect size observed for the knowledge test ( r = 0.574, p = .093) suggests the study was likely underpowered to detect a meaningful 
difference in knowledge acquisition. Nonetheless, these findings are broadly consistent with social cognitive theory ( Bandura, 1986 ), 
which posits that observational learning can be as effective as direct experiential learning when supported by guided reflection. Rather 
than claiming equivalence of learning outcomes, the current findings suggest that the Observer did not demonstrably disadvantage the 
Performer under this design. This finding, however, requires cautious interpretation given the small sample of nine groups ( Lakens, 
2017 ).
Two key factors contributed to these similar learning outcomes. Firstly, the SMR space connected the Performer in the virtual world 
with Observers in the physical space, fostering collaboration and shared experiences ( Benford et al., 1998 ). This connection 
encouraged both Performer and Observer to demonstrate high levels of ownership and engagement in VR simulation. However, 
distinct challenges emerged in this process: Observers couldn't directly enact their thoughts in VR, while Performers sometimes 
struggled with the disconnect between virtual and real worlds. The second essential factor was the guided reflection process. Guided 
reflection enabled both roles, whether through direct interaction with VR or observation, to learn from their experiences and integrate 
their insights into shared understandings ( Yang & Choi, 2023 ). These two factors are interconnected in contributing to comparable 
learning experiences for Performers and Observers. SMR promotes shared experiences and interactions, which becomes foundations 
for meaningful group reflection. Guided reflection, in turn, helps learners analyze and transfer their shared experiences into 
co-constructed understanding. Challenges also emerged in the group reflection process, as Performers faced social pressure from peer 
judgment, while Observers found it challenging to navigate differing opinions. Addressing these role-specific challenges with tailored 
support, such as structured facilitation strategies for Observers and social-emotional scaffolding for Performers, may further enhance 
the effectiveness of VR-mediated group learning.
5.4. Implication
The findings of this study have some implications for research on VR-mediated group learning. The study supports the notion that 
observational experiences can be as effective as immersive ones, but success depends on well-designed instructional strategies, such as 
creating Shared Mixed Reality spaces and incorporating guided group reflection. Additionally, the study revealed distinct challenges 
associated with role assignments, such as Performers' reduced awareness of the physical environment and Observers ’ limited 
engagement in the virtual world. Addressing these challenges with targeted support can improve learning outcomes. Further research 
could explore scaffolding techniques to enhance collaborative learning, particularly in settings with limited access to technology.
The findings of this study also have practical implications for designing VR-mediated group learning. Designing group learning 
environments with limited VR resources can help address programmatic issues, such as equipment shortages, limited time for class 
activities, and teacher overloads, enabling schools and organizations with fewer resources to serve more students. However, 
instructional design is crucial to ensure that learners with varying access to resources achieve similar outcomes. The study shows that 
observational learning in shared physical and virtual spaces, combined with group reflection, can be effective, although a few chal -
lenges remain. For example, different roles and divisions of labor in VR-mediated group learning present unique challenges. The

5.5. Limitations and future directions
This study focused on a specific VR-mediated group learning context, examining how learners experience, reflect, and construct 
knowledge through two distinct roles — Performer and Observer. While the findings contribute to the theoretical understanding and 
practical design of VR-mediated group learning, the scope was limited to nine group cases. Although the current study examined the 
learning outcomes of the two roles (RQ3), the small sample size limits the generalizability of the findings and statistical power to detect 
differences. For example, while no statistically significant differences were observed between Performers and Observers in knowledge 
acquisition and VR behavior performance, a large effect size for knowledge ( r = 0.574, p = .093) and a small effect size for performance 
( r = 0.119, p = .726) suggested uncertainty in the estimates. Larger sample sizes in future research would allow for more precise 
evaluation of potential role-specific differences.
Another limitation concerns the procedure used to assign students to the Performer and Observer roles. In this study, role 
assignment was based on voluntary selection, with random assignment employed in cases where no student volunteered. This 
approach introduces a potential self-selection bias, as students who volunteered for the Performer role may have differed from their 
Observer peers in characteristics that might be relevant to the study outcomes. For example, volunteers may have possessed greater 
technological familiarity with immersive VR environments, higher motivation toward the learning tasks, or greater willingness to 
perform in front of peers, which could influence how they engage and interact with others in the VR-mediated group learning setting. 
However, this cannot be confirmed without baseline measures of factors such as VR experience, motivation, or social confidence. 
Future studies could employ fully randomized role assignment to eliminate self-selection bias and strengthen causal inference 
regarding the differential effects of the Performer and Observer roles in VR-mediated group learning.
In this study, VR-mediated group learning was designed to integrate a shared mixed-reality space with guided group reflection. 
Although these components can be described as two phases, they are inherently interconnected, with experiences in simulation 
potentially shaping the quality and depth of subsequent reflection. Therefore, students' experiences in group VR exposure and sub -
sequent group reflection jointly contributed to their learning outcomes. Future studies can examine the two group learning phases 
separately, as well as in combination, to further examine their individual and combined contributions to learning. Meanwhile, future 
research could further explore how these phases interact over time to influence both individual and collective learning. Investigating 
this connection can provide a more integrated understanding of how immersive VR experiences, when paired with structured 
reflection, support meaningful learning in group settings. For example, future studies could investigate how factors such as group 
cohesion, peer relationships, and role distribution influence learning processes and outcomes. Finally, the study's focus on young 
learners in a Chinese cultural context highlights the need for further research across diverse learning environments and cultural 
settings to ensure generalizability.
