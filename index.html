Computers & Education manuscript draft

Coupling Human and AI Reasoning: Student Agency and Designer Identity in GenAI-Enabled Design Learning

An empirical study of how students couple their own reasoning with AI reasoning, and how this shapes conceptual learning, designer identity, and agency in the Aladdin GenAI-enabled CAD environment.

Abstract

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) is increasingly embedded in computer-aided design (CAD) environments for STEM learning, but less is known about the kinds of student engineers that emerge when learners use AI-generated reasoning during iterative design. This study examined conceptual learning, student engineer types, and student learning agency in a GenAI-supported sustainable building design activity using Aladdin. Data included pre/post conceptual assessments, design logs, AI reasoning traces, learning agency surveys, open-ended responses, and interviews. Students demonstrated significant conceptual gains after the activity. Human-AI trajectory analysis identified five student engineer types that differed in how learners connected technical goals, AI reasoning, and subsequent uptake: AI-guided technical optimizers, technical prompt composers with limited uptake, visual-spatial generators, off-task but technically iterative designers, and off-task weak-coupling explorers. Agency results showed moderate agency overall, with self-adjustment and motivation strongest; exploratory survey-log integration suggested that goal setting and self-cognition were associated with less off-task divergence. ENA-style network analyses further showed that productive student engineer types had denser networks connecting goal-directed prompting, technical parameter use, AI energy reasoning, AI parameter recommendations, and uptake. Findings suggest that effective GenAI-CAD learning depends on students' ability to monitor, select, and transform AI reasoning rather than simply receive AI output.

Keywords: Generative AI; engineering design; sustainable building design; student agency; learning analytics; computer-aided design

1. Introduction

Generative artificial intelligence is changing how students interact with complex design tools. In engineering and STEM learning environments, GenAI can generate design alternatives, explain constraints, recommend technical parameters, and help students move through iterative problem solving. These affordances are especially relevant for sustainable building design, where students must coordinate building form, energy performance, site and climate constraints, solar behavior, and feasibility. However, the educational value of GenAI-supported design does not depend only on whether the system produces useful suggestions. It also depends on how students interpret, evaluate, and incorporate AI-generated reasoning into their own design decisions.

This study focuses on Aladdin, a GenAI-supported CAD environment for sustainable building design. The activity asked high-school students to design an energy-efficient colonial-style house and revise their work through interaction with AI-generated feedback, non-AI energy simulation, and design reasoning. The central argument is that GenAI-CAD interaction produced different student engineer types depending on how students coupled their own goals with AI reasoning. Productive interaction therefore involves more than frequent prompting or surface-level generation; it requires students to set goals, monitor AI explanations, decide what to accept or reject, and transform system suggestions into student-owned design moves.

The paper integrates learning outcome analysis, human-AI design trajectory reconstruction, student engineer type analysis, agency survey and open-ended evidence, and ENA-style network analysis. Three research questions guide the study:

RQ1
Can interacting with a GenAI-based Computer-Aided Design (CAD) tool improve students' conceptual understanding of sustainable building design and STEM engineering education?
RQ2
Through this GenAI-guided CAD process, what student engineer types emerged, and how did those types differ in their human-AI design reasoning?
RQ3
How did interacting with a GenAI-based CAD tool on sustainable building design influence students' agency?

2. Related work

The study draws on research on engineering design as STEM learning, digital design environments that connect design and science reasoning, and emerging work on GenAI in engineering education.

2.1. Engineering design in STEM education

Engineering design has become a critical component of integrative STEM curricula because it gives students opportunities to apply scientific, mathematical, and technological knowledge in authentic problem-solving contexts (Ali & Tse, 2023). Unlike activities that focus only on making artifacts, engineering design requires students to reason about how designed systems work and how they can be improved under specific criteria and constraints. From a design knowledge perspective, this process involves structural knowledge, which describes the components and relationships of objects in a design; functional knowledge, which describes the technical purpose of the design; and behavioral knowledge, which describes the attributes derived from structural knowledge (Gero & Kannengiesser, 2004; Zheng et al., 2020). Engineering design is therefore not only a process of creating physical or digital artifacts, but also a process of connecting design features with scientific mechanisms, system behaviors, and performance outcomes (Dym et al., 2005).

This understanding is reflected in the Next Generation Science Standards, which identify three core components of engineering design: defining and delimiting engineering problems, generating and evaluating multiple possible solutions, and optimizing design solutions through systematic testing and refinement (National Research Council, 2013). These components position engineering design as closely related to scientific inquiry. While scientific inquiry focuses on explaining natural phenomena, engineering design focuses on creating and optimizing solutions. In practice, design challenges often create a need to know, in which students encounter practical problems that require them to investigate scientific principles before making informed decisions (Yata et al., 2020). Similarly, balancing trade-offs among multiple criteria and constraints can prompt students to connect design choices with underlying science concepts (Purzer et al., 2015).

At the high-school level, engineering design can support engagement with science through personally meaningful and socially relevant tasks. For example, Apedoe et al. (2008) implemented a heating/cooling system unit in high-school chemistry, where students designed systems to address personal needs while reasoning about chemical reactions and energy changes. Tang et al. (2026) positioned students in a renewable-energy design unit, where they developed solar adoption plans for their school while balancing constraints and reasoning about sustainability. However, participation in design activities alone does not guarantee expert-like engineering reasoning. Becker and Mentzer (2015) found that high-school students may spend substantially less time than expert engineers on problem scoping, information gathering, feasibility analysis, evaluation, and decision making. These findings indicate that scaffolding is necessary to help students move beyond artifact construction toward systematic, evidence-based design reasoning.

2.2. Digital tools, CAD, and the design-science gap

Digital tools play a critical role in scaffolding the integration of scientific reasoning within engineering design. A persistent challenge in design-based STEM learning is the design-science gap, in which students may focus on constructing or refining artifacts without attending to the scientific principles underlying their designs (Vattam & Kolodner, 2008). Tool-supported environments such as SIMCARS and SHADE have been shown to mediate this gap by providing structured opportunities for students to explore design alternatives, notice relevant variables, and construct causal explanations that connect design decisions to scientific concepts (Vattam & Kolodner, 2008). Building on this perspective, Chao et al. (2017) demonstrated that design tools can shape not only students' design behaviors but also their science learning processes and outcomes, highlighting the active mediating role of tools in design-based learning.

Computer graphics and CAD environments are especially relevant for sustainable building design because they can make otherwise invisible scientific phenomena visible within students' designs (Xie et al., 2023). In energy-efficient building design, students must coordinate structural choices, thermal insulation, solar geometry, HVAC controls, and energy performance. CAD-based and GenAI-supported design environments may expand students' opportunities to generate, analyze, and refine design solutions. Yet, it remains necessary to examine how students use simulation feedback, AI-generated suggestions, and prompt-based interactions to engage in scientific inquiry and make evidence-based design decisions.

2.3. GenAI in engineering education

Recent scholarship suggests that GenAI is beginning to alter engineering education at multiple levels, including learning support, instructional practice, assessment design, and professional skill development. Rather than treating GenAI only as a tool for task completion, existing studies increasingly frame it as part of a changing educational environment in which students interact with AI systems while developing engineering knowledge and judgment. Khatry and Samsami (2025), for example, argue that GenAI can help engineering students receive learning materials, explanations, and practice opportunities that are more responsive to their needs. In technical domains such as circuits, coding, and design, this type of support may help students access alternative explanations, receive timely guidance, and practice with problems adjusted to their current understanding.

The literature also shows that students and instructors experience GenAI in complex and sometimes conflicting ways. Álvarez Ariza et al. (2025) organize GenAI's educational influence across cognitive, affective, and behavioral domains. Students may find GenAI useful for generating ideas, receiving low-pressure support, and improving written or technical work, but they also encounter inaccurate outputs, difficulty writing effective prompts, and worries about becoming overly dependent on AI. Instructors similarly see opportunities for redesigning curricula and saving time, while remaining concerned about plagiarism, cheating, and insufficient faculty preparation.

These debates suggest that GenAI is changing the competencies expected of engineering students. Future engineers may need to communicate with AI systems, evaluate AI-generated outputs, and decide when AI suggestions are technically, ethically, and socially appropriate. Khatry and Samsami (2025) emphasize that engineering education must prepare students for workplaces where AI literacy is increasingly relevant, including technical communication and design decision-making. Paul and Variawa (2025) further identify prompting ability as an emerging skill because the usefulness of AI responses depends heavily on how users define tasks, constraints, and desired formats. However, current research still offers limited process-level evidence about how students coordinate their own design goals with AI-generated reasoning during iterative engineering design. The present study addresses this gap by identifying student engineer types in a GenAI-supported sustainable building design task.

3. Method

The study used a profile-centered mixed-methods learning analytics design with an embedded one-group pretest-posttest component. The design was selected to examine learning outcomes, human-AI design processes, and student learning agency within the same GenAI-CAD learning activity.

3.1. Participants, context, and design task

The study was conducted in a high-school engineering design activity implemented across six class sections. Students completed a two-session sustainable building design task using Aladdin, a GenAI-enabled CAD environment for energy-efficient home design. The task asked students to design a colonial-style house and iteratively reduce its annual energy use through prompting, AI-generated design reasoning, CAD generation, and built-in energy simulation. The required design genre included a colonial-style house; therefore, the term colonial was treated as a task requirement rather than a student-initiated visual or stylistic design choice in the log coding.

Aladdin supports students in generating design solutions, analyzing energy performance, and revising building designs toward a net-zero or low-energy goal. In the activity, students could specify or adjust variables such as location, orientation, roof type, insulation values, window properties, HVAC settings, and solar panel coverage. After generating a house design, students used Aladdin's non-AI Analyze Yearly Building Energy tool to examine monthly and annual heater use, AC use, solar panel output, and net energy use. This sequence positioned GenAI as a design partner whose suggestions needed to be checked against simulation evidence rather than accepted uncritically.

3.2. Procedure

The activity followed the same two-session structure across implementation groups. During the first session, students were introduced to the project, completed a pre-test assessing prior understanding of relevant sustainable building concepts, and began exploring the design task in Aladdin. Students created a Building Design project, set the model to colonial style, and wrote prompts describing the house they wanted to design. They were given general prompt-writing guidance, including the use of concise prompts, combined design ideas, and specific technical terms when appropriate.

During the second session, students continued testing and revising design strategies in relation to the energy-reduction goal. They were encouraged to compare AI-generated reasoning with Aladdin's simulation outputs and to revise prompts or building parameters accordingly. Students documented up to five important design rounds in a worksheet, including their prompts, AI reasoning, and relevant simulation results. At the end of the activity, students completed a post-test with the same conceptual items as the pre-test, a learning agency survey, and open-ended reflection questions about what they learned, how they used AI, and how they understood the benefits and limitations of AI in engineering design. A subset of students also participated in semi-structured interviews lasting approximately 30 to 45 minutes.

3.3. Data sources and measures

Three primary analytic data sources were used. First, pre- and post-assessments measured students' conceptual understanding of sustainable building design and related STEM engineering concepts. The assessment included 12 items addressing thermal insulation and heat transfer, R-value, U-value, greenhouse effect, roof color, solar design, thermostat placement, passive solar design, HVAC strategy, and net-zero design. Second, system log data captured student prompts, AI-generated reasoning, design iterations, and energy-related design metrics. Third, open-ended responses, worksheets, and interview transcripts provided qualitative context for interpreting students' design decisions and human-AI interaction patterns.

3.4. Data analysis

The analytic strategy was organized around the two research questions. RQ1 examined whether students' conceptual understanding changed after the GenAI-supported CAD activity. RQ2 examined what types of designers students became during the activity and how those types unfolded across prompt-based design actions.

3.4.1. RQ1 analysis: Conceptual learning gains

RQ1 was addressed with the matched pre/post assessment sample (n = 68). For each student, a total conceptual-understanding score was computed for the pre-test and post-test, and an improvement score was calculated as the post-test score minus the pre-test score. A one-tailed paired-samples t-test was used to test whether the mean improvement score was significantly greater than zero, reflecting the directional expectation that participation in the GenAI-CAD activity would support conceptual learning.

Two complementary analyses were used to interpret the pre/post pattern. First, Pearson correlation was used to examine the association between students' pre-test and post-test total scores, indicating the stability of relative performance across the activity. Second, McNemar's tests were conducted for each of the 12 assessment items to identify concept-specific shifts in correctness. For each item, the test compared the number of students who changed from incorrect to correct with the number who changed from correct to incorrect. This item-level analysis helped distinguish broad total-score improvement from gains concentrated in particular sustainable building concepts.

3.4.2. RQ2 analysis: Designer profile clustering

RQ2 was first addressed by identifying student designer profiles from the log-data analytic sample (n = 54). Seven student-level features were constructed to represent four dimensions of GenAI-CAD activity: Iterative Engagement, Prompt Sophistication, AI Reasoning Uptake, and Energy Performance. Prior to clustering, all features were z-score standardized (M = 0, SD = 1). Dimension-level equal weighting was then applied so that each of the four dimensions contributed 25% to the weighted feature matrix. The single Iterative Engagement feature received the full 25% weight, whereas each feature within the other three two-feature dimensions received 12.5%.

K-means clustering was conducted on the weighted, standardized feature matrix. Candidate solutions from k = 2 to k = 10 were evaluated using the within-cluster sum of squares elbow pattern and average silhouette score. Solutions containing clusters with fewer than three students were excluded because very small clusters were unlikely to support stable interpretation. Based on these criteria, the three-cluster solution was selected and interpreted as three designer profiles that differed in the configuration of engagement, prompting, uptake of AI reasoning, and energy-performance change.

3.4.3. RQ2 analysis: Sequential design-action analysis

To examine how the designer profiles developed across the task, RQ2 also included a sequential design-action analysis. Each valid design iteration was coded using a scheme developed through a combined deductive and inductive process. Deductively, initial categories were informed by prior work on coding Aladdin CAD log data. Inductively, the codebook was refined through qualitative review of students' GenAI prompts in the present dataset so that the final scheme reflected the design actions observed in this GenAI-supported context.

Table 2. Human-AI design reasoning coding scheme.
Code family Codes Interpretation
Prompt-side indicators Technical parameter; performance goal; visual/spatial focus; off-task divergence How students framed their design requests and whether prompts reflected engineering or surface-level design intentions
AI reasoning indicators Energy reasoning; parameter recommendation; climate/site reasoning; trade-off reasoning; feasibility reasoning What forms of engineering reasoning were generated by the AI tool
Human-AI coupling indicators Direct uptake; selective uptake; transformative uptake; no uptake; weak/off-task coupling Whether and how students incorporated AI reasoning into subsequent design moves

For each iteration, all codes assigned to a student's prompt were combined into an unordered action set, representing the design elements addressed in that attempt. Action sets were then ordered chronologically within each student to create an action-set sequence. Sequence clustering was conducted in TraMineR, with cluster number determined by inspecting the hierarchical dendrogram and by considering the frequency of coded action events, transition rates between states, and within-sequence entropy.

To characterize movement across consecutive design iterations, each adjacent pair of action sets was classified as one of six transition types: repeat/refinement, additive refinement, narrowing, integration move, jump/testing, or pivot/mixed revision. Transition-type rates were computed as the proportion of each transition type within each sequence cluster. These sequential results were used to explain how the designer profiles differed not only in aggregate features, but also in the temporal organization of their prompt-based design work.

Table 3. Prompt-based design strategy coding scheme.

Cumulative incremental Students preserved much of the prior prompt while adding new components or constraints across iterations. Linear accumulation of design requirements toward an evolving goal.
Site exploration Students used location as a major design variable, with multiple distinct cities, states, countries, or climates. Exploration of how place, climate, or geography shaped design possibilities.
Detail-descriptive Students emphasized concrete visual or spatial details such as color, size, doors, windows, roof, pool, or surroundings. Use of GenAI-CAD as a visible design generation and specification tool.

The prompt-strategy coding layer was used as an interpretive complement to the designer-profile and sequence analyses. Whereas the profile model summarized aggregate behavioral indicators, the strategy analysis treated the ordered prompt sequence as a design trajectory. Strategy-type alignment was examined descriptively to clarify how particular prompt trajectories corresponded to the three designer profiles and to the learning patterns reported in the results.

4. Results

4.1. RQ1: Conceptual learning gains

Students demonstrated significant conceptual gains after participating in the GenAI-supported CAD design activity. In the matched sample (n = 68), students' mean score increased from 5.53 (SD = 2.98) at pre-test to 7.07 (SD = 3.98) at post-test on a 12-point assessment. The average raw gain was 1.54 points (SD = 3.71), 95% CI [0.65, 2.44], paired t(67) = 3.43, p = .001, dz = 0.42.

The ANCOVA-style adjusted gain model confirmed this improvement. After controlling for pre-test score and class, the adjusted mean gain was 1.54 points (SE = 0.44), t(61) = 3.54, p < .001, 95% CI [0.67, 2.42]. Pre-test score was a significant covariate, F(1, 61) = 5.92, p = .018, partial eta squared = .088. The class block was not significant, F(5, 61) = 0.67, p = .651. The negative relationship between pre-test score and gain suggests that students with lower baseline scores tended to gain more.

The mixed model further supported the same conclusion. In the model score ~ time + class + (1 | student), the time effect was significant, b = 1.54, SE = 0.45, z = 3.46, p < .001, 95% CI [0.67, 2.42]. The intraclass correlation was .43, indicating substantial stable differences among students and supporting the use of a random-intercept model.

Item-level analysis showed that the total-score gain was distributed unevenly across sustainable building concepts. In the matched sample, the largest item gains were observed for R-value (Q2: 32.4% to 58.8%, +26.5 percentage points), solar panel orientation (Q12: 27.9% to 54.4%, +26.5 percentage points), U-value (Q4: 25.0% to 50.0%, +25.0 percentage points), thermostat setback (Q8: 47.1% to 64.7%, +17.6 percentage points), and passive solar overhangs (Q10: 29.4% to 47.1%, +17.6 percentage points). These gains suggest improvement in several concepts that were directly relevant to the GenAI-CAD house design task.

Aggregating items by conceptual theme showed that the strongest average gains were for solar / passive solar / net-zero concepts (+22.1 percentage points), followed by insulation / R-value / U-value concepts (+17.6 percentage points) and HVAC / thermostat / energy-use concepts (+13.2 percentage points). In contrast, the windows / sunlight / radiant heat theme showed little average improvement (-1.5 percentage points), largely because Q6 declined and Q7 remained unchanged.

Interview evidence helped explain the concepts that students appeared to appropriate during the design activity. Students explicitly discussed insulation as an energy-saving strategy and connected insulation to R-value. For example, one student explained that designers "have to take insulation into consideration" to save energy, and Enil stated that adding insulation increases the R-value. Students also described solar panels as a way to reduce the net energy level, and Raul and Enil connected location to heating, air conditioning, and energy use. These themes align with item-level gains for R-value, U-value, solar-panel orientation, thermostat setback, and net-zero cold-climate insulation.

One exception was Q6, which asked why dark-colored roofs are generally avoided in hot, sunny climates. Correct responses decreased from 73.5% to 63.2%. The answer-choice distribution suggests that the decline was not caused by a single dominant misconception, but by a modest shift toward "dark colors have a higher specific heat" and "I still don't know." Prompt-log review also suggests a possible instructional explanation: many students prompted for roofs, solar panels, colors, or black visual features, but very few prompts connected roof color to hot climate, sunlight, and radiant energy absorption. Thus, roof/color appeared frequently as a visible design feature, but the physics mechanism behind dark-roof heat gain may not have been consolidated.

4.2. RQ2: Student engineer types from human-AI design reasoning trajectories

The final no-colonial coding analysis included 70 valid student/project trajectories. A five-type solution was retained because it had the lowest BIC, high entropy (.988), and no type smaller than seven students. The retained student engineer types were: AI-guided technical optimizers, technical prompt composers with limited uptake, visual-spatial generators, off-task but technically iterative designers, and off-task weak-coupling explorers.

These profiles show that students did not merely differ in the frequency of prompting. They differed in how they coupled their own design intentions with AI-generated reasoning. Productive profiles were characterized by goal-directed prompting, technical parameter use, attention to AI energy/parameter reasoning, and selective or transformative uptake into subsequent design moves.

4.3. Prompt-based design strategies as process trajectories

To explain how students arrived at different student engineer types, the ordered prompt sequences were analyzed as prompt-based design strategies. The most common strategy was detail-descriptive prompting (n = 24), in which students primarily specified visible or spatial features such as color, windows, doors, roof, size, or surrounding environment. The next most common strategy was cumulative incremental prompting (n = 16), in which students preserved much of the previous prompt while adding new constraints or components across iterations. Other strategies included off-task / imaginative exploration (n = 12), site exploration (n = 8), parameter-driven prompting (n = 6), and performance-oriented prompting (n = 3). One case was classified as mixed / low-evidence.

The strategy analysis showed that the model-derived profiles corresponded to recognizable design practices. Strategy-profile alignment was strong, with Cramer's V = .58. Cumulative incremental designers were concentrated in the AI-guided technical optimizer profile: 12 of 16 cumulative incremental students were AI-guided technical optimizers. Parameter-driven designers also aligned with technical profiles, with four of six classified as AI-guided technical optimizers and two as technical prompt composers with limited uptake. In contrast, detail-descriptive designers were primarily visual-spatial generators: 16 of 24 detail-descriptive students belonged to this profile. Off-task / imaginative exploratory designers were concentrated in the two off-task profiles, with seven classified as off-task but technically iterative designers and four as off-task weak-coupling explorers.

The trajectory strip plot illustrates these process differences at the prompt level. Cumulative incremental and parameter-driven trajectories tended to include repeated technical or design-relevant prompt states across multiple iterations. Detail-descriptive trajectories were often shorter and dominated by visible/spatial design states. Site exploration trajectories showed repeated shifts in location or climate, while off-task / imaginative trajectories moved in and out of task-relevant design states.

Strategy-level indicators further clarified how process trajectories related to human-AI coupling. Cumulative incremental designers had high AI reasoning uptake (M = .96), the highest transformative uptake (M = .51), and a strongly technical / AI-reasoning-coupled ENA position (Mdim1 = -1.01). Parameter-driven designers showed the strongest technical network position (Mdim1 = -1.41) and the highest mean learning gain among matched cases (M = 3.25, n = 4), although this assessment comparison is exploratory. Detail-descriptive designers had substantially lower AI reasoning uptake (M = .26) and were positioned on the visual/weak-coupling side of ENA dimension 1 (Mdim1 = .77), despite showing high self-reported agency among matched survey cases. Off-task / imaginative exploratory designers had a high surface-level uptake rate (M = .85) but relatively low transformative uptake (M = .18), suggesting that some task-divergent students continued interaction without deeply transforming AI engineering reasoning into subsequent design decisions.

Exploratory links to pre/post learning and agency should be interpreted cautiously because of limited matching. Among students with matched assessment data, parameter-driven designers showed the largest average gain (M = 3.25, n = 4), followed by cumulative incremental designers (M = 2.60, n = 5) and detail-descriptive designers (M = 2.27, n = 11). Off-task / imaginative exploratory designers showed lower average gain in the matched subset (M = 1.00, n = 2). Agency results showed a different pattern: detail-descriptive designers reported high overall agency in the matched survey subset (M = 4.50, n = 4), suggesting that perceived control may emerge through expressive visual design even when technical AI-reasoning coupling is limited.

4.4. ENA-style type comparison

The ENA-style analysis included 70 student/project networks and 399 design stanzas. ENA dimension 1 explained 53.4% of network variance, while dimension 2 explained 13.7%. Dimension 1 separated technical, goal-directed, AI-reasoning-coupled networks from visual/weak-coupling networks.

AI-guided technical optimizers were positioned on the negative side of dimension 1 (M = -1.188), as were technical prompt composers (M = -0.569) and off-task but technically iterative designers (M = -0.816). In contrast, visual-spatial generators (M = 1.763) and off-task weak-coupling explorers (M = 1.616) were positioned on the positive side. This pattern indicates that dimension 1 captured the extent to which students integrated goal-directed and technical prompting with AI-generated energy and parameter reasoning and subsequent uptake.

The strongest edges differentiating AI-guided technical optimizers from visual-spatial generators included TechPrompt -> AIParameter, TechPrompt -> AIEnergy, GoalPrompt -> TechPrompt, GoalPrompt -> AIParameter, GoalPrompt -> AIEnergy, and VisualSpatial -> SelectiveUptake. Thus, productive design profiles were distinguished not only by the presence of individual behaviors but also by coherent networks connecting goals, technical parameters, AI reasoning, and uptake.

4.5. Conceptual understanding-based ENA

Follow-up ENA comparisons examined whether students with different levels of conceptual understanding showed different human-AI reasoning network structures. Three grouping strategies were used: pre-test conceptual understanding, post-test conceptual understanding, and learning gain.

Pre-test-based ENA suggested that students with stronger initial conceptual understanding already exhibited more integrated human-AI engineering reasoning networks. The high pre-test group (n = 14) had a mean ENA dimension 1 score of -0.825, compared with -0.056 for the low/mid pre-test group (n = 17). Edges stronger for the high pre-test group included TechPrompt -> AIParameter, TechPrompt -> AIEnergy, GoalPrompt -> TechPrompt, GoalPrompt -> AIParameter, TechPrompt -> SelectiveUptake, and TechPrompt -> TransformUptake.

Post-test-based ENA showed a similar pattern. The high post-test understanding group (n = 10) had a mean ENA dimension 1 score of -0.834, compared with -0.221 for the low/mid post-test group (n = 17). Stronger edges for the high post-test group included AIClimate -> SelectiveUptake, TechPrompt -> AIClimate, TechPrompt -> AIParameter, TechPrompt -> AIEnergy, TechPrompt -> AIFeasibility, AIEnergy -> SelectiveUptake, and AIEnergy -> TransformUptake.

Learning gain-based ENA revealed a more nuanced pattern. High-gain students (n = 10) had a mean ENA dimension 1 score of -0.621, compared with -0.386 for low/mid-gain students (n = 14). The strongest high-gain edges involved AI climate/site and feasibility reasoning, including AIClimate -> AIFeasibility, VisualSpatial -> AIClimate, AIParameter -> AIClimate, AIEnergy -> AIClimate, and AIClimate -> SelectiveUptake. This suggests that conceptual growth may be associated with integrating climate/site reasoning and feasibility constraints into design decisions.

4.6. RQ3: Learning agency

Students reported moderate learning agency overall. Across 38 survey responses, the overall agency mean was 3.41 on a 5-point scale (SD = 0.92). The composite mean for key abilities was 3.44, active actions was 3.32, and mental characteristics was 3.50. The strongest individual dimensions were self-adjustment (M = 3.72) and motivation (M = 3.55), whereas participative action (M = 3.16) and goal setting (M = 3.28) were comparatively lower.

Reliability estimates for the two-item dimensions were acceptable to strong. Cronbach's alpha values were .809 for self-cognition, .928 for goal setting, .746 for self-adjustment, .808 for self-reflection, .874 for selective action, .809 for responsible action, .889 for participative action, and .937 for motivation. The overall 17-item agency score showed high internal consistency, alpha = .961.

Exploratory survey-log integration was possible for 12 matched cases. Goal setting was negatively associated with off-task ratio, rho = -.824, p = .001, and self-cognition was also negatively associated with off-task ratio, rho = -.706, p = .010. Self-adjustment showed a positive association with transformative uptake, rho = .555, p = .061. These patterns suggest that students who reported stronger goal setting and self-cognition were less likely to diverge from the task, while students reporting stronger self-adjustment were more likely to transform AI reasoning into subsequent design action.

The mixed-methods joint display suggested that agency took different forms across student engineer types. AI-guided technical optimizers showed high self-adjustment and high log-based uptake. Technical prompt composers reported high agency but showed more limited uptake, suggesting that they may have treated AI as an executor of student-defined design intentions. Visual-spatial generators reported high agency but showed minimal AI reasoning uptake, indicating that subjective control may not always correspond to engineering reasoning coupling. Off-task weak-coupling explorers showed the lowest agency scores and no transformative uptake.

5. Discussion

5.1. Conceptual learning in GenAI-CAD design

The first research question asked whether interacting with a GenAI-based CAD tool could improve students' conceptual understanding of sustainable building design. The results provide affirmative evidence. Students showed a statistically significant increase from pre-test to post-test, with a mean gain of 1.54 points on a 12-item assessment and a medium effect size. This finding suggests that a GenAI-supported design activity can support conceptual learning when students use AI-generated suggestions within a simulation-rich engineering design environment rather than as stand-alone answers.

The gains, however, were selective rather than uniform. The items that improved significantly were concentrated in concepts that were actionable within the Aladdin task, including R-value, U-value, thermostat efficiency, passive solar design, and solar panel orientation. These concepts could be connected directly to design levers that students encountered while prompting, revising, and evaluating houses. By contrast, concepts that were already relatively familiar before the activity or that were less explicitly foregrounded in students' design moves showed little change. This pattern indicates that GenAI-CAD environments do not automatically produce broad conceptual growth; they appear most productive when disciplinary concepts are made available as usable resources for design action.

This result is important because it positions GenAI as part of a broader learning ecology rather than as an isolated tutor. In this study, students were not only reading AI explanations; they were generating designs, checking energy outcomes, and revising prompts. Conceptual learning therefore likely emerged from the coordination of AI reasoning, CAD representation, simulation feedback, and student decision making. The selective item-level gains also point to a design principle for GenAI learning environments: concepts that matter for assessment should also become visible, manipulable, and consequential within the design task.

At the same time, not all concepts improved equally. The windows / sunlight / radiant heat theme showed little average gain, and Q6 on dark-colored roofs declined. This suggests that visual exposure to a feature is not sufficient for conceptual learning. Students frequently manipulated visible design features such as roofs, windows, solar panels, and colors, but prompt-log evidence suggests they rarely connected roof color with hot climates and radiant energy absorption. In other words, students may have treated roof color as an aesthetic attribute rather than as a thermal-performance variable. This pattern supports the need for instruction and tool scaffolds that explicitly connect visible CAD features to underlying energy mechanisms.

5.2. Student designer profiles and human-AI design coupling

The second research question asked what types of designers students became through the GenAI-guided CAD process and how those types unfolded. The cluster analysis identified three profiles: High-Iteration Sophisticated Prompters, Self-Exploratory Testers, and Short-Run Sophistication Improvers. These profiles show that students' engagement with GenAI-CAD differed not only in how much they interacted with the tool, but also in how they configured prompting, AI-reasoning uptake, iteration, and energy-performance change.

High-Iteration Sophisticated Prompters appeared to use GenAI as a sustained design partner. Their many iterations, relatively sophisticated prompts, and stronger uptake of AI reasoning suggest a form of human-AI coupling in which students preserved and extended design goals across attempts. Self-Exploratory Testers, the largest profile, engaged in moderate iteration but showed less systematic coupling between technical prompting, AI reasoning, and subsequent action. Short-Run Sophistication Improvers showed that improvement in prompt sophistication can occur even with fewer iterations, but their limited run length constrained the extent to which AI reasoning could be developed into a sustained design trajectory.

The sequential action analysis adds process-level meaning to these profiles. Students did not simply move from one prompt to another; they formed action-set trajectories that varied in refinement, expansion, narrowing, integration, and jumping across design ideas. Productive GenAI-supported design therefore depends on more than frequent AI use. It requires students to maintain continuity between their own goals, the AI's technical recommendations, the CAD model, and the energy evidence returned by simulation. When that continuity is weak, GenAI can still support exploration, but the exploration may remain visually or procedurally active without becoming conceptually integrated.

This distinction is important for GenAI-supported STEM learning. A student can interact with GenAI frequently without necessarily engaging in disciplinary reasoning. Conversely, productive design learning appears when students monitor AI reasoning, select relevant ideas, and transform those ideas into their own design decisions. Thus, the key learning process is not simply AI assistance, but the student's active coordination of AI reasoning, design constraints, and evolving goals.

5.3. Educational implications

These findings suggest that instruction should help students translate broad performance goals into actionable engineering parameters. Asking for a "net-zero" or "energy-efficient" house expresses an outcome, but it does not necessarily specify the mechanisms by which that outcome might be achieved. Students may need prompts, worksheets, or interface cues that ask them to connect goals with variables such as insulation, glazing, solar-panel orientation, thermostat settings, climate, and passive solar features. Such scaffolds could make the difference between surface-level prompting and disciplinary design reasoning.

The profile results also suggest the value of differentiated support. High-Iteration Sophisticated Prompters may benefit from tools that support comparison across many design alternatives and help them evaluate trade-offs. Self-Exploratory Testers may need prompts that make AI reasoning uptake more explicit, such as asking which AI suggestion they accepted, rejected, or modified and why. Short-Run Sophistication Improvers may need supports that encourage additional cycles of testing and revision so that early gains in prompt quality can develop into sustained engineering inquiry.

For GenAI-CAD tool design, the results point toward interfaces that make reasoning-action links visible. For example, when AI reasoning mentions insulation, solar orientation, or climate conditions, the system could invite students to decide whether and how to incorporate that reasoning into the next design move. Dashboards could also show how prompts, AI reasoning, and energy outcomes change over time. These features would support students in treating AI as a source of ideas to interrogate and transform, rather than as an automatic design generator.

5.4. Limitations and future research

Several limitations should be considered. First, the study used a one-group pretest-posttest design, so the learning-gain results should be interpreted as evidence of improvement following the activity rather than as causal evidence that GenAI alone produced the gains. Second, the log-data sample was smaller than the assessment sample, and the designer profiles should be validated with larger cohorts and additional implementations. Third, the action-code scheme was developed for this GenAI-supported sustainable building task; future work should examine whether the same codes and transition patterns generalize to other engineering domains and AI-CAD systems.

Future research should combine stronger comparison designs with richer process measures. Experimental or quasi-experimental studies could test whether scaffolds for goal setting, AI-reasoning evaluation, and parameter-based revision improve both conceptual learning and designer-profile development. Additional qualitative work could examine how students interpret AI explanations, decide when to trust or reject them, and connect them to simulation evidence. Such work would deepen understanding of how GenAI can support engineering learning while keeping students' disciplinary reasoning and design agency at the center of the activity.
