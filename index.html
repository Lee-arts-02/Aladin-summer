Computers & Education manuscript draft

Coupling Human and AI Reasoning: Student Agency and Designer Identity in GenAI-Enabled Design Learning

Abstract

1 Introduction

GenAI tools are increasingly used in STEM learning contexts to support concept learning and problem solving, yet their educational value remains uncertain because students may use them as shortcuts rather than scaffolds (Cao et al., 2023; Otto et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024). This tension is especially salient in engineering design education, where students must apply scientific ideas to open-ended problems with multiple variables and constraints (Fortus et al., 2004; Xie et al., 2018). Yet this integration is difficult: the scientific principles that shape design performance are often less visible than surface-level features such as form, size, or appearance, and applying these principles requires students to understand how variables interact within a system (Xie et al., 2023). GenAI-assisted CAD may help address this challenge by combining visual and performance feedback with adaptive support as students explore, evaluate, and revise design solutions.

However, it remains unclear how students learn from this support. Since engineering design problems rarely have a single correct solution, students may engage with GenAI in ways that support different design trajectories, ranging from output generation to more systematic performance-oriented revision. In this context, a better-performing artifact should not be taken as direct evidence of stronger conceptual understanding or more productive design strategies, a concern consistent with studies showing that GenAI can improve task performance without necessarily improving underlying learning processes (Fan et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024). Studies of AI assistance further suggest that learning depends partly on how responsibility is distributed between AI and learners, and whether students remain agency in evaluating and revising AI-supported work (Darvishi et al., 2024; Lai et al., 2026). Although GenAI is increasingly discussed in engineering education, much of this work has examined higher education students' adoption and perceptions or the broader integration of GenAI into engineering teaching (Canavate et al., 2025; Ovi et al., 2025). Evidence from engineering students also suggests that perceived study efficiencydo not necessarily translate into changes in academic performance (L. Fan et al., 2025). What remains less clear is whether GenAI-assisted CAD is associated with gains in students' conceptual understanding, and how students' interactions with GenAI are reflected in different engineering design strategies.

To address these gaps, the present study examined students' conceptual learning and engineering design strategies in a GenAI-supported CAD environment. Students completed a net-zero energy house design task in which they used GenAI while revising their CAD models. We asked:

RQ1. Can interacting with a GenAI-based Computer-Aided Design (CAD) tool, improve high school students’ conceptual understanding of sustainable building design and STEM engineering education?

RQ2. Through this Gen-AI-supported CAD process, what different types of designers did students become, and how?

2 Related Work

2.1 Engineering design in STEM education

Engineering design has become a critical component of integrative STEM curricula because it provides students with opportunities to apply scientific, mathematical, and technological knowledge in authentic problem-solving contexts (Ali & Tse, 2023). Unlike activities that focus only on making artifacts, engineering design requires students to reason about how designed systems work and how they can be improved under specific criteria and constraints. From a design knowledge perspective, this process involves structural knowledge, which describes the components and relationships of objects in a design; functional knowledge, which describes the technical purpose of the design; and behavioral knowledge, which describes the attributes derived from structural knowledge (Gero & Kannengiesser, 2004; Zheng et al., 2020). These forms of knowledge indicate that engineering design is not only a process of creating physical or digital artifacts, but also a process of connecting design features with scientific mechanisms, system behaviors, and performance outcomes (Dym et al., 2005).

This understanding is reflected in the Next Generation Science Standards, which identify three core components of engineering design: defining and delimiting engineering problems, generating and evaluating multiple possible solutions, and optimizing design solutions through systematic testing and refinement (National Research Council, 2013). These components position engineering design as closely related to scientific inquiry. While scientific inquiry focuses on explaining natural phenomena, engineering design focuses on creating and optimizing solutions. In practice, however, these two processes are deeply intertwined. Design challenges often create a “need to know,” in which students encounter design problems that require them to investigate scientific principles before making informed decisions (Yata et al., 2020). Similarly, balancing trade-offs among multiple criteria and constraints can prompt students to connect design choices with underlying science concepts (Purzer et al., 2015). In this sense, engineering design can provide an authentic purpose for inquiry by helping students understand why scientific knowledge matters for solving practical problems.

At the high school level, engineering design provides students with opportunities to engage in complex, socially relevant problem-solving while connecting to core scientific concepts. For example, Apedoe et al. (2008) implemented a Heating/Cooling System unit in high school chemistry, where students designed systems to address personal needs while reasoning about chemical reactions and energy changes. Tang et al. (2025) positioned students in a renewable energy design unit, where they developed solar adoption plans for their school, balancing constraints and reasoning about sustainability. These studies suggest that engineering design can support engagement with science through personal meaningful tasks. However, participation alone does not guarantee expert-like engineering reasoning. Becker and Mentzer (2015) found that high school students may spend substantially less time than expert engineers on problem scoping, information gathering, feasibility analysis, evaluation, and decision making. These findings indicate that scaffolding is necessary to help students move beyond artifact construction toward systematic, evidence-based design reasoning.

Digital tools play a critical role in scaffolding the integration of scientific reasoning within engineering design. A persistent challenge in design-based STEM learning is the “design–science gap” (Vattam & Kolodner, 2008), in which students may focus on constructing or refining artifacts without attending to the scientific principles underlying their designs. Tool-supported environments, such as SIMCARS and SHADE, have been shown to mediate this gap by providing structured opportunities for students to explore design alternatives, notice relevant variables, and construct causal explanations that connect design decisions to scientific concepts (Vattam & Kolodner, 2008). Building on this perspective, Chao et al. (2017) demonstrated that design tools can shape not only students’ design behaviors but also their science learning processes and outcomes, highlighting the active mediating role of tools in design-based learning. Similarly, Xie et al. (2023) illustrated that computer graphics can make otherwise invisible scientific phenomena visible within students’ designs, and GenAI-based systems offer additional affordances for supporting human–computer collaboration. Building on this line of work, CAD-based and GenAI-supported design environments may further expand students’ opportunities to generate, analyze, and refine design solutions. Yet, it remains necessary to examine how students use simulation feedback, AI-generated suggestions, and prompt-based interactions to engage in scientific inquiry and make evidence-based design decisions.

2.2 GenAI in Engineering Design

Recent scholarship suggests that generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) is beginning to alter engineering education at multiple levels, including learning support, instructional practice, assessment design, and professional skill development. Rather than treating GenAI only as a tool for task completion, existing studies increasingly frame it as part of a changing educational environment in which students interact with AI systems while developing engineering knowledge and judgment. In this context, one major area of discussion is the use of GenAI to support more individualized learning. Khatry and Samsami (2025) argue that GenAI can help engineering students receive learning materials, explanations, and practice opportunities that are more responsive to their needs. In technical domains such as circuits, coding, and design, this type of support may help students access alternative explanations, receive timely guidance, and practice with problems adjusted to their current understanding. The authors also connect GenAI to competency-based education, which emphasizes demonstrable skills and real-world application rather than time-bound learning (Khatry and Samsami 2025). In this context, GenAI can provide targeted feedback on practical engineering tasks, including engineering design and circuit analysis. Thus, existing work suggests that GenAI may expand assessment from a primarily evaluative function toward a more supportive role in helping students develop applied engineering competencies.

The literature also shows that students and instructors experience GenAI in complex and sometimes conflicting ways. Álvarez Ariza, Benitez Restrepo, and Hernández Hernández (2025) organize GenAI’s educational influence across cognitive, affective, and behavioral domains. In higher education, the cognitive domain is especially visible, as students use GenAI to understand technical concepts, write or revise code, and troubleshoot problems. At the same time, students report both benefits and concerns. They may find GenAI useful for generating ideas, receiving low-pressure support, and improving written or technical work, but they also encounter inaccurate outputs, difficulty writing effective prompts, and worries about becoming overly dependent on AI. Instructors similarly see opportunities for redesigning curricula and saving time, while remaining concerned about plagiarism, cheating, and insufficient faculty preparation.

These debates suggest that GenAI is changing the competencies expected of engineering students. Future engineers may need to communicate with AI systems, evaluate AI-generated outputs, and decide when AI suggestions are technically, ethically, and socially appropriate. Khatry and Samsami (2025) emphasize that engineering education must prepare students for workplaces where AI literacy is increasingly relevant, including technical communication and design decision-making. Paul and Variawa (2025) further identify prompting ability as an important emerging skill, because the usefulness of AI responses depends heavily on how users define tasks, constraints, and desired formats. Their work also suggests that assessment should shift toward students’ ability to critique, interpret, and responsibly apply AI-generated outputs rather than simply produce answers.

Overall, these studies indicate that GenAI is producing changes across engineering learning, teaching, and assessment. Existing research has identified potential benefits in individualized support, iterative feedback, active learning, and AI literacy development. However, it also points to unresolved challenges, including unreliable AI outputs, unclear expectations for student AI literacy, academic integrity concerns, and limited preparation for instructors.

3 Methodology

3.1 Participants

Participants were recruited from five class sections at a public high school in North Carolina, USA. Four roster documents were labeled as Physical Science sections; the remaining roster was provided as part of the same classroom implementation record. Across the five sections, the rosters included 118 students taught by three teachers. One teacher taught three sections (Blocks 1, 2, and 4; 70 students in total), a second teacher taught one section (31 students), and a third teacher taught one section (17 students).

Students were primarily enrolled in Grade 10, although the rosters also included students in Grades 9, 11, and 12. The activity was implemented during regular classroom instruction as part of the students' science course.

3.2 Learning Environment and Procedure - Aladdin

Learning Objectives

Aladdin is a GenAI-enabled computer-aided design (CAD) tool that helps learners visualize scientific concepts and operationalize inquiry practices in engineering design (Xie et al., 2023). In this activity, learners use GenAI to design an eco-friendly house with the goal of reducing energy consumption as much as possible. The activity enhances inquiry in design by engaging learners in the process of generating design ideas, analyzing energy performance through simulation, and revising their solutions toward the net-zero goal. Through this process, learners practice using GenAI in engineering design, a critical skill aligned with the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS). By assessing, refining, and changing their designs with GenAI, learners can also develop an understanding of the potential benefits and limitations of introducing GenAI into engineering design.

Core Activity Supported by Aladdin

Generate a design solution

Students begin by using GenAI in Aladdin to generate an initial net-zero house design. They create a Building Design project and write a prompt describing the house they want to design. This step introduces prompting as a way for students to translate design ideas into AI-generated building solutions. To support this process, students are provided with general prompt-writing guidelines, such as writing concise prompts, combining multiple design ideas when appropriate, and using specific technical terms. Although the activity constrains students to a colonial-style house format, they can still adjust key design variables in their prompts, such as location, orientation, roof type, insulation values, window properties, HVAC settings, and solar panel coverage.

Analyze the design solution

After generating a house design, students use Aladdin’s built-in non-AI energy simulation tool, Analyze Yearly Building Energy, to evaluate the design. The tool calculates the house’s monthly and annual energy performance, including heater use, AC use, solar panel output, and net energy use. These results provide quantitative evidence for students to evaluate whether the AI-generated design moves toward the net-zero goal. This step encourages multi-source verification by asking students to compare AI-generated design suggestions with evidence from Aladdin’s built-in non-AI energy simulation.

Iteratively Improve the Design Solution

Based on the simulation results, students revise their prompts and generate improved versions of the house to reduce its energy usage. Students are introduced to the default variables that Aladdin assumes when these variables are not specified in prompts, which also points them to multiple ways of further reducing energy use. In this sense, students learn how to modify design parameters and iteratively improve their house design toward the net-zero goal.

Document interaction with AI

Students are required to document their prompts, AI’s reasoning, and relevant simulation results in the worksheet for up to five important design rounds. This documentation not only helps students keep track of their progress toward the net-zero goal, but also makes the human-AI design process visible. By reviewing these records, students can more easily examine their design decisions, pay attention to AI’s reasoning, and reflect on the implications of AI-generated suggestions.

Reflection

At the end of the activity, students complete a final reflection. They share their Aladdin project link and reflect on three questions: what science and engineering concepts they learned from AI, what strategies they learned for designing toward the net-zero goal, and what they see as the advantages and limitations of using AI in engineering design. This final step connects the design experience to broader learning about sustainable engineering and responsible GenAI use.

3.3 Experiment Design

Participating students completed a two-session engineering design activity using Aladdin, a generative AI-supported design environment for energy-efficient home design. The activity asked students to work toward the goal of reducing a house’s energy cost by testing and refining different design strategies. Each implementation group included three class blocks and followed the same two-session structure. During the first session, students were introduced to the project, completed a pre-survey assessing their prior knowledge of relevant engineering concepts, and began exploring the design task in Aladdin and set the model to colonial style. During the second session, students continued working on the design task by testing and revising design strategies in relation to the energy-cost reduction goal. At the end of the second session, students completed a post-survey that used the same knowledge questions as the pre-survey to examine changes in conceptual understanding after the Aladdin-supported design activity.

In addition to the pre- and post-surveys, a subset of students was invited to participate in individual semi-structured interviews after completing the two sessions. Each interview lasted approximately 30 to 45 minutes and focused on students’ experiences using generative AI in Aladdin for engineering design.

An additional student learning agency survey was also administered after the design activity. This survey was intended to capture students’ reflections on their own role, decision-making, and self-regulation while interacting with generative AI during the design process.

Pre- and Post-Test

The pre- and post tests included the same 12 questions designed to assess students’ prior understanding of concepts relevant to energy-efficient home design. The questions mapped onto four conceptual areas: thermal insulation and heat transfer, solar geometry and radiation, HVAC controls and strategy, and sustainable engineering goals. The full pre- and post-survey are included in Appendix.

Interview Design

The semi-structured interview protocol was organized around three main primary questions (add areas?). The main interview questions then focused on three topics: concept learning, design strategies and problem solving, and critical evaluation. First, students were asked about the science and engineering concepts they learned through interacting with AI. Second, students were asked about the design strategies they learned from AI when designing toward the net-zero goal. Third, students were asked to evaluate the advantages and limitations of using AI in engineering design. Below is the interview questions:

What was your first impression of using AI for a complex engineering task like this?

What strategies have you learned from AI in designing towards the net-zero goal?

What is your opinion about the pros and cons of using AI in engineering design?

Student Learning Agency Survey

The student learning agency survey was adapted from the Student Learning Agency in Generative AI-supported Contexts scale developed by Xia et al. (2025). This scale was developed to measure student agency specifically in learning environments that involve generative AI., rather than relying on general learning agency measures. The survey in this study was designed to examine how students perceived their own role, decision-making, and self-regulation while using Aladdin.

The survey included Likert-scale closed-ended items organized around multiple dimensions of student agency: self-cognition, goal setting, self-adjustment, self-reflection, selective action, responsible action, participative action, motivation, self-efficacy, and volition. These dimensions correspond to Xia et al.’s framework, which groups student learning agency into key abilities, active actions, and essential mental characteristics in generative AI-supported contexts (2025). In this study, these items were used to examine how students understood their strengths and challenges, set design goals, revised prompts or AI-generated content, reflected on AI interactions, decided when to rely on AI support, and maintained responsibility for their own design decisions.

The agency survey also included four open-ended questions:

When working on your design with AI, how did you plan, adjust, and reflect on your work? Please give one example.

How did you decide what to do yourself and what support to use during your design process? Please give one example.

How did you evaluate your own ability to use generative AI during your design process? Please give one example.

How do you see your role when interacting with generative AI in designing your house?

Together, the pre/post surveys, student learning agency survey, classroom observations, and interviews were designed to provide complementary data sources for examining students’ conceptual learning and their experiences of agency in AI-supported engineering design.

3.4 Data Collection and Measures

3.4.1 Platform Log Data

Log data were collected automatically through the GenAI-supported CAD design platform (Aladdin), which recorded each student's interactions across all design attempts. For each iteration, the platform log captured the student's prompt submitted to the AI system, the AI-generated reasoning in response to the prompt, the resulting net energy cost of the building design, and the iteration number. The full log dataset contained records from 70 active students across 399 total design iterations. Because the research questions concern how students engaged with the iterative design-revise cycle over time, only students who completed more than one iteration were retained for analysis. Applying this criterion yielded a final analytic sample of 54 students.

3.4.2 Pre- and Post-Test Assessments

Pre- and post-test assessments used the same set of 12 building energy concept problems, administered before and after the GenAI-CAD design task, respectively. The original datasets included 124 pre-test records and 74 post-test records. Participants were matched across the two datasets using usernames. A total of 68 students appeared in both and were included in the paired analysis sample. Individual improvement scores were calculated by subtracting each student's pre-test total score from their post-test total score. These assessments were used to address RQ1 and were not included as inputs to the clustering algorithm described in Section 2.6.

The assessment was designed to capture conceptual understanding that students would need when evaluating and revising sustainable building designs in Aladdin. The 12 items were organized around four conceptual directions that correspond to the major energy mechanisms embedded in the design task: thermal insulation and heat transfer, solar geometry and radiation, HVAC controls and strategy, and sustainable engineering goals. This alignment allowed the pre/post test to measure not only whether students improved overall, but also which conceptual areas were most responsive to the GenAI-CAD activity.

3.5 Feature Construction

Seven student-level features were constructed from the raw platform log data to characterize each student's engagement across four behavioral dimensions: iterative engagement, prompt sophistication (PSI), AI reasoning uptake, and energy performance. All features were computed directly from raw text and numeric log values, no pre-coded values or externally derived scores were used at any stage. Table 1 shows how each dimension is calculated.

Prompt sophistication: qualitative coding to quantitative features

Prompt sophistication was quantified at the iteration level using a Prompt Sophistication Index (PSI). Each student prompt was coded for three components: specificity, conceptual/computational breadth (CCB), and reasoning orientation. The iteration-level score was calculated as PSIi = specificityi + CCBi + reasoning orientationi. Two student-level features were then derived from the sequence of iteration-level PSI scores. Mean PSI represented the average sophistication of a student's prompts across all valid iterations. PSI growth represented the ordinary least squares linear regression slope of PSIi regressed on iteration number, capturing whether a student's prompting became more sophisticated over time.

Table 2. Prompt Sophistication Index coding rubric.
PSI component Coding focus Example indicators in student prompts Score contribution
Specificity Degree to which the prompt specifies concrete design parameters rather than a general request. Location, orientation, roof type, R-value, U-value, HVAC setting, solar panel placement, window dimensions. 0-2
Conceptual/computational breadth (CCB) Degree to which the prompt coordinates multiple sustainable-building concepts or design variables. Combining envelope, solar, HVAC, climate, window, shading, or energy-performance elements in the same prompt. 0-2
Reasoning orientation Degree to which the prompt expresses a rationale, performance goal, trade-off, or causal mechanism. Requests to reduce heating load, improve net-zero performance, use insulation to reduce heat transfer, or adjust design choices because of energy consequences. 0-2

AI reasoning uptake: qualitative coding to quantitative features

AI reasoning uptake was quantified by estimating the extent to which students incorporated AI-generated reasoning into subsequent prompts. For each iteration, uptake combined semantic similarity and concept overlap: uptakei = 0.5 × semantic uptakei + 0.5 × concept overlapi. Semantic uptake was computed using TF-IDF cosine similarity between the AI reasoning and the student's subsequent prompt, while concept overlap measured the extent to which key concepts from the AI reasoning reappeared in the next student prompt. Two student-level features were derived from this sequence. Mean uptake represented the average uptake of AI reasoning across iterations. Uptake growth represented the ordinary least squares linear regression slope of uptakei regressed on iteration number, capturing whether students increasingly incorporated AI reasoning as the design task progressed.

3.6 Data Analysis

Two research questions guided the analytic strategy, each addressed by a distinct set of methods.

3.6.1 Pre/Post Learning Outcome Analysis (RQ1)

To address that question, whether interacting with the Aladdin improved students' conceptual understanding of sustainable building design. Three complementary statistical analyses were conducted on the paired pre/post sample (n = 68). First, a one-tailed paired-samples t-test was used to examine whether the mean improvement score (post-test minus pre-test total score) was significantly greater than zero. Second, Pearson correlation was used to examine the relationship between individual pre-test and post-test total scores, providing an indication of the relative stability of student performance across the intervention. Third, McNemar's test was conducted at the item level for each of the 12 assessment items. For each item, the test compared the number of students who switched from incorrect to correct against those who switched from correct to incorrect, thereby identifying which specific concept areas showed significant shifts in correctness, rather than treating learning gain as a single undifferentiated outcome.

3.6.2 Designer Profile Clustering (RQ2)

To identify the types of designers students became across the platform, a K-Means clustering analysis was conducted on the seven student-level features described in Section 2.5, applied to the log-data analytic sample (n = 54). A clustering approach was adopted because the first component of RQ2 concerns the identification of student subgroups that differ in their overall configuration of behaviors across multiple dimensions simultaneously.

Prior to clustering, all seven features were z-score standardized (M = 0, SD = 1) to place them on a comparable numeric scale. To prevent any single behavioral dimension from dominating the clustering solution by virtue of having more features, dimension-level equal weighting was applied after standardization: each of the four behavioral dimensions - Iterative Engagement, Prompt Sophistication, AI Reasoning Uptake, and Energy Performance - contributed equally (25%) to the weighted feature matrix used for clustering. Because Iterative Engagement is represented by a single feature, it received the full 25% weight; each of the two features within the remaining three dimensions received 12.5% each.

K-Means clustering was then applied to this weighted, standardized matrix, with the number of clusters k evaluated from k = 2 to k = 10. Two diagnostics guided model selection. First, the WCSS elbow plot was inspected to identify the point at which the reduction in WCSS flattened as k increased. Second, the average silhouette score was used to evaluate the separation quality of each solution, with higher values indicating that students were more clearly assigned to their own cluster relative to adjacent clusters. Solutions in which any cluster contained fewer than three students were excluded as unreliable. Based on these criteria, the k = 3 solution was selected for the analytic sample, as it yielded the highest silhouette score among stable solutions. Cluster labels were assigned to describe how each group engaged with the GenAI-CAD tool.

3.6.3 Design Action Analysis (RQ2)

To address the question of how each designer type's behavior unfolded sequentially across design iterations, a sequential analysis was conducted using an action-level coding schema applied at the iteration level.

The action-code scheme was developed through a combined deductive and inductive process. Deductively, initial categories were informed by Singh et al. (2022), who developed a set of manually defined design-action categories for coding Aladdin CAD log data. The codebook was subsequently refined through inductive review: categories were removed or added based on a manually qualitative examination of students' generative AI prompts in the present dataset, ensuring that the final scheme reflected the specific action types observed in this GenAI-supported design context. The resulting scheme comprised 14 codes, summarized in Table 2.

For each iteration, all codes assigned to a student's prompt were combined into an unordered action set, reflecting the design elements addressed within that attempt. These iteration-level action sets were then ordered chronologically within each student to produce an action-set sequence representing the trajectory of design focus across the full task. Sequential and clustering analysis was conducted using TraMineR. Cluster number was determined by inspecting a hierarchical dendrogram, with cluster quality evaluated using frequency of coded action events, transition rates between states, and within-sequence entropy.

To characterize how students moved between prompted action sets across consecutive iterations, each pair of adjacent action sets was classified into one of six transition types: repeat/refinement (identical sets), additive refinement (codes only added), narrowing (codes only removed), integration move (the subsequent set met a predefined integrated energy configuration), jump/testing (no code overlap between sets), and pivot/mixed revision (all remaining cases). Transition type rates were then computed as the proportion of each type out of all observed transitions within each cluster.

4 Results

4.1 RQ1: Can Interacting with a GenAI-Based CAD Tool Improve Students' Conceptual Understanding of Sustainable Building Design?

Overall, students demonstrated significant improvement in conceptual understanding of building energy concepts after engaging with the Aladdin GenAI tool. Students entered the program with a mean score of 5.53 out of 12 (SD = 2.98), or approximately 46% correct, reflecting limited prior knowledge. By the post-test, the mean rose to 7.07 out of 12 (SD = 3.98), or 59% correct, a gain of 1.54 points on average. A one-tailed paired-samples t-test confirmed that this improvement was statistically significant, t(67) = 3.43, p < .001, indicating that the observed gain was unlikely to be attributable to chance. At the individual level, 54.4% of students improved their scores, 19.1% showed no change, and 26.5% declined.

4.2 RQ2: Through the GenAI-Guided CAD Process, What Different Types of Designers Did Students Become, and How?

4.2.1 Descriptive Analysis of Design Engagement Features

Prior to clustering, descriptive statistics were examined for the seven student-level features computed from the log data of the analytic sample (n = 54). Across the full sample, students completed a mean of 7.09 design iterations (SD = 4.27), indicating considerable variability in behavioral persistence. Mean Prompt Sophistication Index (PSI) was 1.22 out of 6.0 (SD = 1.12), suggesting that students' prompts were generally low in specificity, conceptual breadth, and reasoning orientation, with wide individual variation. Mean AI reasoning uptake was 0.34 out of 2.0 (SD = 0.20), reflecting that most students incorporated only limited concepts from AI feedback into their subsequent prompts.

4.2.2 K-Means Clustering: Three Designer Profile Types

To identify distinct types of designers, K-Means clustering was applied to the seven standardized and dimension-weighted features. Cluster solutions from k = 2 to k = 10 were evaluated using the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS) elbow plot and average silhouette scores.

The k = 3 solution was selected as it yielded the highest silhouette score among solutions with no cluster smaller than three students. The resulting three clusters contained 9, 36, and 9 students respectively, and are described below.

Cluster 1: High-Iteration Sophisticated Prompters (n = 9)

Students in this cluster were distinguished by the highest number of design iterations among the three groups (M = 14.78, SD = 2.95), reflecting sustained behavioral engagement with the design-revise cycle across the task. Their mean PSI was the highest of the three clusters (M = 1.97, SD = 1.22), indicating relatively greater specificity and conceptual breadth in prompting, though PSI remained stable over time (PSI growth: M = 0.05, SD = 0.07), suggesting that these students entered the task with an already-developed prompting approach rather than developing it progressively. Mean AI reasoning uptake was also the highest across clusters (M = 0.51, SD = 0.08), indicating that these students more consistently incorporated AI feedback into subsequent prompts than their peers.

Cluster 2: Self-Exploratory Testers (n = 36)

The largest cluster, comprising two-thirds of the analytic sample, showed moderate levels of iterative engagement (M = 6.22 iterations, SD = 2.23) and low prompt sophistication (M = 1.09, SD = 1.15). PSI showed some growth over the task (M = 0.23, SD = 0.30), but the overall level remained low, indicating that while some students in this group became slightly more specific in their prompts over time, prompts were generally surface-level throughout. AI reasoning uptake was the lowest of the three clusters (M = 0.30, SD = 0.21), suggesting limited responsiveness to the AI-generated design feedback. This cluster represents the modal student design pattern in this sample.

Cluster 3: Short-Run Sophistication Improvers (n = 9)

Students in this cluster completed the fewest design iterations (M = 2.89, SD = 0.93), but showed the most rapid growth in prompt sophistication over the course of those iterations (PSI growth: M = 0.94, SD = 0.45), by far the highest PSI growth across all three clusters. Although their overall mean PSI was comparable to Cluster 2 (M = 0.98, SD = 0.48), the steep upward trajectory indicates that within a small number of attempts, these students substantially increased the specificity and conceptual scope of their design requests. Uptake growth was similarly the highest of the three clusters (M = 0.32, SD = 0.19), suggesting that even in their limited number of iterations, these students showed an increasing tendency to incorporate AI reasoning into subsequent prompts. This pattern may reflect a rapid learning curve within a compressed design process.

4.2.3 Design Action Analysis

The design action analysis examined how the three clusters differed in their design-process patterns.

The figure X presents the frequency with which each of the 14 action codes appeared in prompted action sets across the three clusters. Permutation chi-square tests indicated that 11 of the 14 codes differed significantly across clusters (all p ≤ .024), while Color / appearance (C; p = .356, V = .07), Analysis / simulation (A; p = 1.000, V = .00), and Off-topic (X; p = .199, V = .10) showed no statistically significant cluster differences.

The three clusters differed most clearly in the breadth and structural orientation of their prompted codes. Cluster1 students engaged with building envelope components at substantially higher rates than either Cluster 2 or Cluster 3 students, with Roof (65.4% vs. 33.0% and 23.1%), Insulation (61.7% vs. 29.0% and 23.1%), and Wall (44.4% vs. 16.1% and 0.0%) all yielding medium effect sizes (V = .32, .32, and .34, respectively). This pattern suggests that Cluster 1 students attended to multiple structural systems as a matter of course rather than selectively.

Cluster 2 students, by contrast, were characterized by a relatively high frequency of Outcome / net-zero goal (12.9%) and Door (12.1%) prompting, codes that were rare or absent in both Cluster 1 and Cluster 3, pointing to a subset of students who framed their prompts around explicit performance targets or programmatic specifications. Meanwhile, Cluster 3 students were distinguished by the narrowness of their prompted repertoire: while Solar / battery remained their dominant code (57.7%), structural and system codes such as Wall and HVAC were entirely absent (0.0%), a stark contrast to Cluster 1 where these codes appeared in nearly half or more of all iterations.

The most frequent action sets differed across clusters. In Cluster1, the two most frequent action sets were F+W+R+S+I (13%) and F+W+N+R+S+I+H+C+G+B (10%). In Cluster 2, the most frequent action sets were G (13%). In Cluster 3, the most frequent sets were S (15%), F+S+I+C (12%), R+S+G (12%), and G (12%).

The depth means how many codes appear at the same time of iteration. Action-set depth differed significantly across clusters (χ2 = 73.62, p = .001, V = .31), with Cluster 1 students producing depth-4+ action sets in 79% of their iterations. This stands in marked contrast to Cluster 3, where single-code prompts accounted for 38% of iterations and depth-0 sets, reflecting no identifiable prompted action, reached 12%, the highest rate across all three clusters. Cluster 2 occupied an intermediate position, with iterations distributed relatively evenly across depth categories. These findings suggest that Cluster 1 students routinely integrated multiple design dimensions within a single prompt, whereas Cluster 3 students most often addressed one dimension at a time or produced prompts that could not be attributed to any specific design action.

The transition type analysis revealed systematic differences in how students moved between prompted action sets across iterations. Cluster 1 students were characterized by a dominant repeat/refinement pattern, with 63% of transitions involving identical action sets across consecutive iterations, suggesting that these students sustained engagement with the same design configuration rather than shifting focus. Cluster 2 students showed the highest rate of additive refinement (29%), indicating a tendency to incrementally expand their action sets by introducing new codes without abandoning existing ones. Cluster 3 students, by contrast, exhibited the highest rates of both integration move (35%) and jump/testing (12%), suggesting that when Cluster 3 students did change their prompts, they were more likely to either leap toward a qualitatively different configuration or produce transitions with no code overlap across consecutive iterations.

To illustrate cluster-level prompting behavior, a medoid sequence was identified for each cluster. The medoid was defined as the student whose prompted action-set sequence had the highest mean DTW similarity to all other sequences within the same cluster, making it the most centrally representative trajectory in the group. The representative sequences illustrate these patterns at the level of individual prompting behavior. The Cluster 1 medoid student (Student H) began with a location-anchored prompt and systematically accumulated design specifications across iterations, retaining solar, insulation, and window codes from iteration 2 onward and introducing HVAC only in iteration 8. This trajectory reflects the incremental pattern dominant in Cluster 1: the core prompt remained stable while new elements were selectively incorporated. The Cluster 2 medoid student (Student N) repeated an identical location-only prompt across the first three iterations before introducing solar panels in iteration 4 and appending insulation and HVAC in subsequent prompts. Her final iteration, however, dropped these additions entirely and shifted to "Window Insulation," consistent with the partial uptake and late pivot characteristic of Cluster 2's additive-then-diverge pattern. The Cluster 3 medoid student (Student D) completed only three iterations, moving from a bare location prompt to solar panels and then to a multi-component specification combining roof, solar, and insulation in a single step.

5 Discussion

5.1 Conceptual learning in GenAI-CAD design

The first research question asked whether interacting with a GenAI-based CAD tool could improve students' conceptual understanding of sustainable building design. The results provide affirmative evidence: students demonstrated statistically significant gains from pre- to post-test, with the largest improvements concentrated in concepts that were directly actionable within the design task. This pattern suggests that GenAI-supported CAD activity can support conceptual learning when students encounter scientific ideas as design levers rather than as decontextualized facts.

The selective pattern of gains is theoretically important. Items related to R-value, U-value, thermostat efficiency, passive solar design, and solar-panel orientation improved, whereas concepts that were already comparatively familiar or less explicitly connected to students' design moves changed less. This suggests that GenAI-CAD environments do not automatically produce uniform conceptual growth. Instead, learning appears most likely when concepts are visible, manipulable, and consequential in the design process.

The decline on the dark-roof item further illustrates this point. Students often manipulated visible design features such as roofs, colors, windows, and solar panels, but visible manipulation alone did not ensure understanding of the underlying energy mechanism. For Computer & Education audiences, this finding underscores the need to design GenAI learning environments that connect generated artifacts to explanatory mechanisms and simulation evidence.

5.2 Designer types as patterns of human-AI reasoning

The second research question examined what types of designers students became and how those types unfolded through the GenAI-CAD process. The three-cluster solution shows that students differed not simply in the amount of tool use, but in how they configured iteration, prompt sophistication, AI-reasoning uptake, and energy-performance change. High-Iteration Sophisticated Prompters appeared to engage in sustained human-AI design reasoning, Self-Exploratory Testers showed moderate but less systematic engagement, and Short-Run Sophistication Improvers demonstrated rapid prompt growth within fewer design cycles.

The design-action analysis clarifies how these profiles emerged over time. Productive engagement was characterized by continuity across prompts, action sets, and simulation feedback. Students who repeatedly refined or expanded action sets were better positioned to connect AI suggestions to design constraints and energy goals. In contrast, less integrated trajectories could still involve active prompting, but the activity was less likely to become sustained engineering reasoning.

These findings suggest that productive GenAI use in engineering design should be understood as human-AI reasoning coupling. The educational value of GenAI does not come only from generating a design or providing an explanation; it depends on whether students interpret AI reasoning, select relevant ideas, and transform those ideas into subsequent design decisions.

5.3 Implications for instruction and tool design

Instructionally, students may need scaffolds that help them translate broad goals, such as net-zero energy, into actionable engineering parameters. Reflection prompts, worksheets, and interface cues can ask students to identify which AI suggestions they accepted, modified, or rejected and to justify those decisions using simulation evidence.

For tool design, the results point toward interfaces that make reasoning-action links visible. A GenAI-CAD environment could highlight when AI reasoning refers to insulation, solar orientation, climate, or feasibility, and then prompt students to decide whether and how to incorporate that reasoning into the next design move. Such support would keep students' design agency and disciplinary reasoning at the center of AI-assisted design learning.

5.4 Limitations and future research

Several limitations should be noted. The study used a one-group pre/post design, so learning gains should be interpreted as improvement following participation rather than as causal evidence that GenAI alone produced the gains. The designer profiles were based on a modest log-data sample and should be validated with larger cohorts. In addition, the action-code scheme was developed for this sustainable building task and should be tested in other GenAI-supported design contexts.

Future research should use stronger comparison designs and richer process measures to examine how scaffolds for goal setting, AI-reasoning evaluation, and parameter-based revision affect both conceptual learning and designer-profile development. Such work can clarify how GenAI tools support engineering learning while preserving students' role as active designers and reasoners.
